\begin{abstract}
A leaderboard that reports cost beside accuracy is read through its Pareto frontier. This is the set of systems that no cheaper system beats. The frontier is drawn from point estimates on a sample of tasks, so it is an estimate, and no leaderboard reports how uncertain it is. We give a simultaneous confidence band for the frontier curve. We also give a confidence set for its members that contains the whole true frontier with probability at least $1-\alpha$ in large samples. The band is proved for fixed or per-task costs. The set is proved for fixed costs, and for per-task costs when the true mean costs are distinct. A tie-robust set, a step-down on paired cost and accuracy comparisons, drops that condition at the price of a larger set. All guarantees are for the submitted systems and for tasks drawn independently from the benchmark's task process. They say nothing about tuning on public tasks. We check the methods on known truth in \NumValScenarios{} scenarios and report where they fail. The failures include nearly tied costs, cheaper near-copies of other systems, and heavy-tailed costs. The band also fails when tasks are strongly clustered or cost logs are missing. From the public call logs of the Holistic Agent Leaderboard (HAL), we rebuild per-task costs for \NumHalSystemsPerTaskCost{} runs on \NumHalBoardsPerTask{} benchmarks, and we reprice the run totals of \NumHalSystemsFixedCost{} runs on an eleventh. HAL's cost totals double count nested trace spans on \NumDupRunsAffected{} of \NumDupRunsTotal{} runs. Counting each call once changes HAL's frontier on \NumFlipBoardsChangedCounting{} benchmarks. Counting and pricing together change it on \NumFlipBoardsChanged{} of \NumFlipBoards{}. With uncertainty added, the share of systems that cannot be excluded from the frontier runs from \NumHalSetShareMinPct\% to \NumHalSetShareMaxPct\% across HAL boards. On a second leaderboard, HELM Capabilities, it runs from \NumHelmSetShareMinPct\% to \NumHelmSetShareMaxPct\%, and its boards have \NumHelmTasksMin{} to \NumHelmTasksMax{} tasks. Those shares assume that tasks are independent and that one run is a system's average. If the true standard errors were 1.5 times ours, the HAL standard sets would hold \NumInflHalSetOneFive{} of \NumInflHalSystems{} systems instead of \NumInflHalSetOne{}. Some pairs of an entrant and the holder it must beat are certified as new records on a random half of the tasks. On the other half, the mean lead is positive for \NumReplHalCertPairsLeadPos{} of \NumReplHalCertPairs{} such pairs on HAL and \NumReplHelmCertPairsLeadPos{} of \NumReplHelmCertPairs{} on HELM. The pairs are few, many are certified in only one or two splits, and the intervals are wide. The certificates and a winner's-curse correction for frontier members are in the appendices, each stated under its own null. The methods are in the open-source package \texttt{honestboard}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

More and more leaderboards for AI agents report what a run costs beside how often it succeeds. The Holistic Agent Leaderboard (HAL) \citep{kapoor2025hal} does this for every benchmark it hosts. It draws the cost-accuracy picture the way an economist would. A system is worth looking at only if no cheaper system does at least as well. The systems that pass this test form the Pareto frontier. HAL's own write-up stresses how few systems sit on it. Readers and the teams that build agents act on that picture. A system on the frontier is a reasonable one to consider at its price. A system below it usually is not.

The picture is drawn from point estimates. Each system's accuracy is its success rate on a sample of tasks: \NumAssistantbenchTasks{} on AssistantBench, \NumCorebenchHardTasks{} on CORE-Bench hard, \NumTaubenchAirlineTasks{} on tau-bench airline and on the SWE-bench Verified mini split. With samples that small, the rate has a standard error of several points. The frontier is a function of those rates, so it moves when the rates move. A system just below the frontier might sit on it under another draw of tasks. A system on it might not. HAL reports no uncertainty for its frontiers.

This paper supplies that uncertainty. It answers four questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item How high, and how low, can the frontier curve really be? This curve is the best accuracy available at each cost. And which systems cannot be excluded from the frontier at a stated confidence level?
\item How wrong is the plain frontier when the truth is known, and where do the guarantees fail?
\item What is the cheapest system a reader cannot yet rule out as the best?
\item How many tasks would a board need before its frontier is resolved?
\end{enumerate}
It also audits HAL with per-task costs rebuilt from its call logs. Two appendices give certificates for new frontier entrants and a winner's-curse correction for frontier members.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A simultaneous confidence band for the frontier curve itself. It is valid with fixed or per-task costs, and with no condition on ties among the true mean costs (Section~\ref{sec:band}).
\item A confidence set for the frontier's members. It contains the whole true frontier with probability at least $1-\alpha$ in large samples, and it is proved for fixed costs and for per-task costs without ties. We also give a tie-robust version that drops the condition on ties, and exact finite-sample sets (Section~\ref{sec:set}).
\item Resolution numbers, meaning the task count at which a board would start to resolve its own frontier. Also two descriptive summaries: a bootstrap frontier stability, and the cheapest unresolved competitor of the best (Sections~\ref{sec:resolve} and~\ref{sec:summaries}).
\item A known-truth validation in \NumValScenarios{} scenarios. The scenarios include near-copy systems, outcome-dependent costs, nearly tied costs, heavy-tailed costs, missing cost logs and clustered tasks. The validation also has a stability check on held-out halves of real boards, and an account of where the guarantees fail (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).
\item An audit of HAL with consistent costing: each model call counted once, one price table, the double count quantified for every run, the effects of counting and of pricing on HAL's published frontiers reported separately, and the sensitivity of the audit to the costing rules, to clustered tasks and to run-to-run variation (Sections~\ref{sec:data}, \ref{sec:audit} and~\ref{sec:limits}).
\item A second leaderboard, HELM Capabilities, audited with the same tools, and a fresh-task replication of the entrant certificates on both leaderboards (Section~\ref{sec:helm}).
\item In the appendices, two certificates for a new frontier entrant with chain-wise error control. One is a record claim, and one is a certificate of Pareto membership under a stated null. The appendices also give a proof that no certificate built on the entrant's strongest rival alone can hold its level, and the winner's-curse correction for frontier members.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What the paper does not claim.}
The tools are adapted. They are not invented from nothing. The set is Hsu's multiple-comparisons-with-the-best method. For each system, only the systems that cost no more count as rivals, and the margin is taken jointly. The exact sets are paired sign tests with Bonferroni. The certificates are the selective tests of a companion paper, with a different conditioning event. Our inference is conditional on the submitted systems. It takes the tasks as draws from the benchmark's task process. It does not account for tuning on public tasks, or for which systems were submitted (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).

\paragraph{Related work.}
Earlier work has treated uncertainty in leaderboard rankings in several ways. These include confidence sets for ranks \citep{mogstad2024ranks,neuhof2026rankintervals,neuhof2026ranking}, confidence sets of the best \citep{hsu1981mcb,hansen2011mcs}, the winner's curse of a selected system \citep{andrews2024winners,bakshi2026winners}, and standard errors for language-model evaluations \citep{miller2024errorbars,bowyer2025clt,madaan2024variance}. The observed frontier is a free-disposal-hull estimator. Econometrics has developed inference for such frontiers, for sampled firms with exactly measured inputs and outputs. This includes the asymptotics of the free-disposal-hull estimator \citep{park2000fdh} and the bootstrap for nonparametric frontier models \citep{simar1998bootstrap,simar2000state}. Tests of stochastic dominance \citep{linton2005sd} ask a different question. Finance studies the sampling error of an efficient frontier \citep{brittenjones1999}. Evolutionary optimisation studies the sampling error of Pareto fronts under noisy objectives \citep{fieldsend2005noisy}. Here the units are systems. Their coordinates, accuracy and cost, are both estimated from the same tasks, and the tasks are shared across units. Others have also studied how to identify a Pareto set from noisy observations when the analyst chooses what to sample next. Examples are Pareto-set identification with $(\epsilon,\delta)$ guarantees from bandit feedback \citep{auer2016pareto}, active learning of the Pareto front \citep{zuluaga2013pal}, and multi-objective ranking and selection in simulation \citep{applegate2020mors}. Cost has entered leaderboard design through HAL \citep{kapoor2025hal}, through the argument that agent evaluations should report it \citep{kapoor2024agents}, and through the cost-of-pass framework \citep{erol2025costofpass}. All of these work from point estimates. To our knowledge, no earlier work gives a published cost-accuracy frontier a simultaneous confidence band and a confidence set for its members. The literatures above are close, but a systematic comparison with them is beyond one paper.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

A board has $K$ systems evaluated on the same $T$ tasks. System $k$ scores $y_{kt}\in[0,1]$ on task $t$. On most benchmarks this is a pass or a fail. On some it is a partial score. Its accuracy is the mean $\bar y_k=T^{-1}\sum_t y_{kt}$, which estimates $\mu_k=\E\,y_{kt}$. Its cost is either one number per system, $c_k$ (the price of the run divided by $T$), or one number per task, $c_{kt}$. In the second case $\bar c_k$ is the mean, and it estimates $\gamma_k=\E\,c_{kt}$. On HAL the per-task costs come from the call logs (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

System $j$ \emph{dominates} system $i$ when $c_j\le c_i$ and $\bar y_j\ge\bar y_i$ with at least one of the two strict. The \emph{observed frontier} is the set of systems that no other system dominates. Two systems that are equal in both cost and accuracy do not dominate each other, so both can be on the frontier. This is the only tie convention we use, and we apply it throughout. The \emph{true frontier} is the same set, with $\mu_k$ and $\gamma_k$ in place of $\bar y_k$ and $\bar c_k$. A system is truly undominated when no other system dominates it in this sense.

\paragraph{What is estimated.}
We take the systems as given. Everything here is conditional on the $K$ submitted systems. It says nothing about systems that were tried and not submitted, or tuned on the public tasks. The tasks are draws from the benchmark's task process. They are independent across tasks, but dependent across systems on the same task, because hard tasks are hard for everyone. The target is each system's expected accuracy $\mu_k$ on new tasks from that process. For a fixed set of tasks, the empirical frontier is exactly determined. The uncertainty we describe arises only because the tasks are a sample. The target is a benchmark population, not a deployment population. A result about new kinds of tasks, other repositories or sites, or real use is not covered. Clustered tasks are where the result is most at risk (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Cost is the priced token usage of reproducing the run, in dollars per task under one price table. Its target is the expected value $\gamma_k$ under the task process. So a frontier says which systems are cheapest to reproduce at a given accuracy, at the prices of the table. It does not say which were the best buys when they were run. The price table is a fixed list. A system's cost is the priced token count of its run, and this varies with the tasks drawn, so costs are random where prices are not. Three things are not modelled: tuning of a system on the public tasks, run-to-run variation of a system beyond independent task-level noise, and changes in prices over time.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The observed frontier and why it is not enough}
The observed frontier is what every cost-aware leaderboard draws. It is a function of $K$ estimated accuracies, each with a standard error of order $T^{-1/2}$. It is also a discontinuous function: moving one accuracy by a hair can add or remove a system. Section~\ref{sec:validation} measures how often it is wrong when the truth is known. In short, with \NumValR{} simulated boards of 20 systems on 50 tasks, \NumValNaiveFalseA\% of the systems on the observed frontier are truly dominated, and \NumValNaiveMissedA\% of the true frontier is missed.

\subsection{A confidence band for the frontier curve}
\label{sec:band}
A cost-aware leaderboard draws a curve. At each cost $x$, the curve shows the best accuracy available from any system whose cost is at most $x$,
\[
A(x)=\max_{j:\,\gamma_j\le x}\mu_j,
\]
where $\gamma_j$ is the true mean cost of system $j$. The curve is a staircase that steps up at the cost of each frontier member. The plain frontier is this curve drawn with the sample means $\bar y_j$ and $\bar c_j$ in place of $\mu_j$ and $\gamma_j$. A simultaneous band for $A$ follows from a simultaneous band for all $2K$ means at once. Let $\se_j$ and $\mathrm{sg}_j$ be the standard errors of $\bar y_j$ and $\bar c_j$. The standard error is zero for a cost that is a fixed number. Resample the tasks, taking outcomes and costs together. For each resample, take the largest of $|\bar y_j^{(b)}-\bar y_j|/\se_j$ and $|\bar c_j^{(b)}-\bar c_j|/\mathrm{sg}_j$ over all systems. Let $c_{\mathrm{band}}$ be the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of these largest values over the resamples. Each system then has an accuracy interval $[\bar y_j\mp c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j]$ and a cost interval $[\bar c_j\mp c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j]$. The lower and upper edges of the band are
\[
L(x)=\max_{j:\ \bar c_j+c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j\le x}(\bar y_j-c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j),\qquad
U(x)=\max_{j:\ \bar c_j-c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j\le x}(\bar y_j+c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j).
\]
A system enters the lower edge only when its cost is surely within the budget $x$. It enters the upper edge when its cost possibly is. So neither edge relies on getting the cost order right.
\begin{proposition}[band]
\label{prop:band}
Let the task vectors of outcomes and costs be i.i.d., with every random accuracy and cost having positive variance. Let $K$ be fixed, and let each true accuracy $\mu_j$ be fixed in $(0,1)$ as $T\to\infty$. Then
\[
\Pr\bigl(L(x)\le A(x)\le U(x)\ \text{for every}\ x\ge\min_j\gamma_j\bigr)\ \ge\ 1-\alpha-o(1),
\]
with costs fixed or measured per task, and with no condition on ties among the true mean costs. Below the cheapest true cost, $A(x)$ is a maximum over an empty set.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $E$ be the event that every $\mu_j$ lies in its accuracy interval and every $\gamma_j$ in its cost interval. If $\bar c_j+c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j\le x$ then on $E$ $\gamma_j\le x$, so $\bar y_j-c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j\le\mu_j\le A(x)$. So $L(x)\le A(x)$. If $\gamma_j\le x$ then on $E$ $\bar c_j-c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j\le\gamma_j\le x$, so the maximizer $j$ of $\mu_j$ over $\{\gamma_j\le x\}$ enters $U(x)$ with $\bar y_j+c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j\ge\mu_j=A(x)$. So $U(x)\ge A(x)$. Both hold for every $x$ on $E$. We use the multivariate central limit theorem and the consistency of the bootstrap for the mean vector \citep{bickel1981bootstrap}. Slutsky's theorem applies because the standard errors converge in probability. So the bootstrap law of the maximal studentised deviation converges to that of the maximum of the absolute values of a Gaussian vector whose coordinates have positive variance. Its distribution function is continuous and strictly increasing. So $\Pr(E)\ge1-\alpha-o(1)$.
\end{proof}
The proposition is asymptotic, with each $\mu_j$ fixed in $(0,1)$. It does not protect the systems with few passes or few failures. A bootstrap-$t$ interval covers too rarely for them. It shrinks to a single point when a system passes every task, or fails every task, in the sample. A system with true accuracy $0.02$ on $50$ tasks fails every task a third of the time. On a small board of four systems, the band built from bootstrap-$t$ intervals alone covered the true curve only $62\%$ of the time. So for pass/fail outcomes, a system with fewer than ten passes or ten failures gets a Clopper--Pearson interval at level $\alpha/(2K)$ in place of the bootstrap interval. The maximal deviation is taken over the other systems and all costs, at level $\alpha/2$. When every system is small, the maximal deviation is taken at level $\alpha$, with Clopper--Pearson at $\alpha/K$. When no system is small, there is no Clopper--Pearson part. The Clopper--Pearson half is exact, whatever the data select. Each interval covers with probability at least $1-\alpha/(2K)$, whether or not its system is small. So the $K$ of them fail together with probability at most $\alpha/2$. The maximal-deviation half is asymptotic. The proposition covers it when every system is regular, which means that the expected passes and failures both tend to infinity, so that no system is small with probability tending to one. For a sparse system, or one whose expected passes lie near ten, the data decide the switch between the two intervals. Then the total $1-\alpha$ is a design target that the simulations support (Table~\ref{tab:valfrontier}). It is not a theorem. We designed this rule for the low-accuracy boards of HAL (AssistantBench, SciCode).
Nothing in the proof uses the cost order. So the band does not need the cost order to be recoverable, but the frontier set of Section~\ref{sec:set} does. The price is width. A system whose cost is uncertain is allowed into the upper edge long before it is allowed into the lower one. The band is a statement about the curve. The set is a statement about its members. The two use different statistics, and neither implies the other. The band is what to draw on a leaderboard page (Figure~\ref{fig:frontiers}). Its width on HAL, as a median over the evaluated costs, runs from \NumHalBandWidthMin{} points (\NumHalBandWidthMinName) to \NumHalBandWidthMax{} points (\NumHalBandWidthMaxName). In simulation it covers the true curve \NumValBandCovMin{} to \NumValBandCovMax{} of the time in \NumValScenariosFirst{} of the \NumValScenarios{} scenarios. These include fixed costs, nearly tied costs and the low-accuracy board (Tables~\ref{tab:valfrontier} and~\ref{tab:realism}). It falls short in two of the last three rows of Table~\ref{tab:realism}. The coverage is \NumValScMissingCostLogsBand{} when failed tasks have no logged cost. It is \NumValScStronglyClusteredTasksBand{} when tasks are strongly clustered, and resampling the groups brings it back to \NumValScStronglyClusteredTasksBandCluster{} (Section~\ref{sec:stress}). On most boards the coverage is above the nominal level. This is because the band takes one maximal deviation over all $2K$ means, for a curve that depends on one maximum at each cost. So the band is conservative by construction. A first version of the band trusted the sample cost order. It covered the true curve only \NumValBandNaiveCovMin{} to \NumValBandNaiveCovMax\% of the time on the simulated boards of Tables~\ref{tab:valfrontier} and~\ref{tab:realism}. This is why the construction above lets a system into an edge only on the strength of its cost interval.


\subsection{A confidence set for the frontier}
\label{sec:set}
Call $j$ a \emph{rival} of $i$ when $c_j\le c_i$ and $j\ne i$. A rival is any system that could dominate $i$ on cost. For each pair, let $\hat\se_{ij}$ be the paired standard error of $\bar y_j-\bar y_i$. We compute it from the task-level covariance of the two outcome series. Let
\[
G_{ij}=\frac{\bar y_j-\bar y_i}{\hat\se_{ij}}
\]
be how far $j$ sits above $i$ in standard-error units. Two systems with identical outcomes on every task have a zero paired standard error and a zero gap. We set the standard error to $10^{-12}$, so that $G_{ij}=0$ and the pair never excludes a system. System $i$ is on the observed frontier exactly when $G_{ij}<0$ for every rival (with the tie conventions). The confidence set keeps $i$ unless some rival beats it by more than a simultaneous margin:
\[
\hat F_\alpha=\Bigl\{\,i:\ \max_{j\ \text{rival of}\ i} G_{ij}\le c_\alpha\Bigr\}.
\]
The margin is a bootstrap quantile. Resample the $T$ tasks with replacement $B$ times. In resample $b$, write $\bar y_k^{(b)}$ for the accuracies. When costs are per task, write $\bar c_k^{(b)}$ for the costs, so that the rival sets are resampled too. Let
\[
M_i^{(b)}=\max_{j\ \text{rival of}\ i\ \text{in resample}\ b}\ \frac{(\bar y_j^{(b)}-\bar y_i^{(b)})-(\bar y_j-\bar y_i)}{\hat\se_{ij}},
\]
which is the largest centred, studentised deviation of any rival from $i$. Let $M^{(b)}=\max_i M_i^{(b)}$ be the largest such deviation on the board. The margin is the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of $M^{(b)}$ over resamples, $c_\alpha=\max(0,\,q_{1-\alpha}(M))$. This is Hsu's multiple-comparisons-with-the-best method \citep{hsu1981mcb}, as used for the confidence set of the best in \citet{opoku2026records}. Here the rival set of each system is cut down to the systems that cost no more, and the margin is taken jointly over systems. Two properties are immediate. First, $\hat F_\alpha$ always contains the observed frontier, because a frontier member has every $G_{ij}<0\le c_\alpha$. Second, it shrinks toward the observed frontier as $T$ grows, because the margin is in standard-error units.

\begin{proposition}[the set contains the true frontier]
\label{prop:retention}
Fix the costs. Let the task vectors be independent and identically distributed with finite variances, with the population variance of $y_{jt}-y_{it}$ positive for every pair compared, and let $K$ be fixed as $T\to\infty$. Let $F$ be the set of systems that no rival beats in true accuracy, the $i$ with $\mu_j\le\mu_i$ for every rival $j$. It contains the true frontier of Section~\ref{sec:setting}, which also drops a system tied in true accuracy with a strictly cheaper one. Then
\[
\Pr\bigl(F\subseteq\hat F_\alpha\bigr)\ \ge\ 1-\alpha-o(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $i\in F$ and any rival $j$, $\mu_j-\mu_i\le0$, so $G_{ij}\le Z_{ij}:=\{(\bar y_j-\bar y_i)-(\mu_j-\mu_i)\}/\hat\se_{ij}$. So $\max_{i\in F}\max_j G_{ij}\le\max_{i\in F}\max_j Z_{ij}\le\max_i\max_j Z_{ij}=:Z$. Each maximum is over the system's rivals, and these sets do not change with the data when costs are fixed. By the multivariate central limit theorem, the centred, scaled mean vector is asymptotically Gaussian. The nonparametric bootstrap of the mean vector is consistent \citep{bickel1981bootstrap}. Both $Z$ and $M$ divide by the sample standard errors $\hat\se_{ij}$, which converge in probability to their population values. So by Slutsky's theorem and the continuous mapping theorem, $Z$ and the bootstrap $M$ share the limit law of the maximum of the coordinates of a Gaussian vector with positive coordinate variances. The distribution function of this law is continuous and strictly increasing. So $c_\alpha$ converges to that law's $(1-\alpha)$ quantile, and $\Pr(Z>c_\alpha)\to\alpha$. So $\Pr(F\not\subseteq\hat F_\alpha)=\Pr(\max_{i\in F}\max_j G_{ij}>c_\alpha)\le\Pr(Z>c_\alpha)\le\alpha+o(1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This is a confidence set for the frontier as a set. With probability about $1-\alpha$, every truly undominated system is kept at once. Two smaller sets with weaker, per-system guarantees come from the same resamples. The first uses the largest per-system quantile, $\max_i q_{1-\alpha}(M_i)$, as the margin. The second uses each system's own quantile $q_{1-\alpha}(M_i)$. Each keeps a given truly undominated system with probability about $1-\alpha$. Section~\ref{sec:validation} reports all three against the alternatives a practitioner might reach for instead. On HAL the three sets hold \NumHalFrontierSet{}, \NumHalBoardwideSet{} and \NumHalPerSystemSet{} systems. We call this joint set the frontier set. When we compare it with the tie-robust set, we also call it the standard set.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[per-task costs]
\label{prop:randomcost}
Let costs be measured per task, with the task vectors of outcomes and costs i.i.d., every accuracy and cost having positive variance and $y_{jt}-y_{it}$ having positive variance for every pair. Let the true mean costs $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_K$ be distinct. Let $F$ be defined from the true accuracies and true mean costs. Then the frontier set built with per-task costs resampled jointly with the outcomes satisfies $\Pr(F\subseteq\hat F_\alpha)\ge1-\alpha-o(1)$. (The band of Proposition~\ref{prop:band} needs no such condition.)
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With distinct true mean costs, the sample mean costs recover the true cost order with probability tending to one. So do the resampled mean costs, within every resample except a vanishing share, since each is $\sqrt T$-consistent for its target. On that event every rival set, real and resampled, equals the true rival set. The statistics then coincide with those of the fixed-cost construction with the true rival sets, so Proposition~\ref{prop:retention} applies. Ties in true mean cost are the case the proof excludes. There, a rival set stays random in the limit. Proposition~\ref{prop:tierobust} below removes the condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
On HAL the per-task and fixed-cost versions give identical sets on \NumHalBoardsSetSameFixed{} of the \NumHalBoardsPerTask{} boards with per-task costs. Cost differences between neighbouring systems are mostly many times their standard error, so on these boards cost uncertainty does not move the set. That is a fact about HAL's cost spread, not a general property. The next paragraph shows what happens without it. Reruns of one configuration have the same true cost. This breaks the distinctness condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:randomcost} for those pairs, but in the harmless direction. Whether the sample order puts a twin among a system's rivals or not, the twin is a true rival. So a wrong order can only withhold evidence from the set.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{When true costs tie.}
\label{sec:ties}
Proposition~\ref{prop:randomcost} needs distinct true mean costs, and near ties are what break it. Take a system that is truly cheaper than a rival by one per cent, with the rival truly more accurate. The system is on the true frontier, because the rival does not cost less. But when the rival's sample cost happens to come out lower, the standard set treats the rival as a rival. It may then exclude the system on its evidence. In the nearly tied scenario of Section~\ref{sec:stress}, true mean costs are a few per cent apart and per-task costs have a coefficient of variation of 0.8. There the standard set contains the whole true frontier in only \NumValScNearTiedCostsJoint{} of simulated boards (Table~\ref{tab:realism}), against the nominal 0.95. The same failure arises when a near-copy of another system costs a few per cent less than the original (\NumValScCheaperNearCopiesJoint{}). The remedy has two parts. We exclude a system only when another system is shown to be strictly cheaper and strictly more accurate. And we use one critical value for both kinds of evidence.

The procedure is a step-down in the sense of Romano and Wolf \citep{romano2005stepdown}. It works on two families of one-sided hypotheses, one pair for every ordered pair of systems. $C_{ij}$ says that $j$ is not strictly cheaper than $i$, $\gamma_j\ge\gamma_i$. $A_{ij}$ says that $j$ is not more accurate than $i$, $\mu_j\le\mu_i$. Let $D_{ij}=(\bar c_i-\bar c_j)/\hat\se^{c}_{ij}$ be the studentised mean cost difference. Here $\hat\se^{c}_{ij}$ is the paired standard error of the cost difference. Costs move together across tasks, so it is much smaller than the sum of the two unpaired standard errors. $G_{ij}$ is as before. Start with no hypothesis rejected. At each step, let $c$ be the $(1-\alpha)$ bootstrap quantile of the largest centred, studentised deviation over the hypotheses that still stand. Reject every standing $C_{ij}$ with $D_{ij}>c$ and every standing $A_{ij}$ with $G_{ij}>c$, recompute $c$, and repeat until nothing changes. The tie-robust set excludes $i$ when some $j$ has both $C_{ij}$ and $A_{ij}$ rejected.

\begin{proposition}[tie-robust set]
\label{prop:tierobust}
Let costs be measured per task, with the task vectors of outcomes and costs i.i.d., every accuracy and cost having positive variance and the paired differences of accuracies and of costs having positive variance for every pair. There is no condition on ties among the true mean costs. Let $F$ be the set of systems $i$ with $\mu_j\le\mu_i$ for every $j\ne i$ with $\gamma_j\le\gamma_i$. The tie-robust set contains $F$ with probability at least $1-\alpha-o(1)$, and it contains the observed frontier.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal N$ be the set of true hypotheses, and let $W$ be the event that none of them is rejected. A true hypothesis has a studentised statistic no larger than its centred version, so rejecting it needs its centred deviation to exceed the current $c$. Let $k$ be the first step at which a true hypothesis would be rejected. Every hypothesis rejected before step $k$ was false, so the hypotheses standing at step $k$ include all of $\mathcal N$. The critical value $c_k$ is then at least the $(1-\alpha)$ bootstrap quantile of the largest centred deviation over $\mathcal N$. By the multivariate central limit theorem and the consistency of the bootstrap for the mean vector \citep{bickel1981bootstrap}, that quantile converges to the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of the maximum of a Gaussian vector with positive coordinate variances. The distribution function of that maximum is continuous and strictly increasing. So the probability that a true hypothesis is rejected at step $k$ is at most $\alpha+o(1)$, and $\Pr(W)\ge1-\alpha-o(1)$. On $W$, an excluded $i$ has some $j$ with $C_{ij}$ and $A_{ij}$ both false, that is $\gamma_j<\gamma_i$ and $\mu_j>\mu_i$, so $i\notin F$. If $i$ is on the observed frontier and both $C_{ij}$ and $A_{ij}$ were rejected, then $\bar c_j<\bar c_i$ and $\bar y_j>\bar y_i$, so $j$ dominates $i$ in the sample. This is impossible.
\end{proof}
The proof uses nothing about how the cost and accuracy sampling errors depend on each other. We also checked the set in a demanding case by simulation. Every system has the same true accuracy, so every system is in $F$ and any exclusion is an error. True costs are spread so that their gaps are of the order of the critical value. In 3 of the \NumStressSettings{} settings, failed tasks cost three times as much as passed ones. The set kept all of $F$ in \NumStressSdMin{} to \NumStressSdMax{} of boards, against the nominal 0.95 (supplement S12).

A \emph{gated} variant tests $A_{ij}$ only once $C_{ij}$ has been rejected. The gate means that fewer hypotheses are tested at the start, so the sets are smaller, at most \NumValRobGatedExcessMax\% above the standard set, against at most \NumValRobExcessMax\% for the set above. We do not use it, because the argument above does not cover it. With the gate, the number of hypotheses at risk grows as accuracy hypotheses are exposed, so the critical value is not monotone across steps. Also, which hypotheses are exposed depends on the cost differences, and these are correlated with the accuracy differences when failed tasks cost more. In simulation it kept the whole true frontier in at least \NumValGatedBaseJointMin{} of boards in every one of the \NumValScenarios{} scenarios, and in \NumStressGatedMin{} to \NumStressGatedMax{} in the demanding case. So the proof, not the evidence, is what is missing.

The price is size. When costs are tied more closely than their noise, no system is shown to be cheaper than another. Nothing is excluded, and the set is the whole board. That is the honest answer: the data cannot say which systems are cheaper, so they cannot resolve the frontier. The set does not shrink to the true frontier when true costs are exactly tied. A truly dominated system with an exactly tied dominating twin is kept at any $T$, because the twin is almost never shown to be cheaper. Where costs do differ, the set is larger than the standard set by up to \NumValRobExcessMax\% in simulation. That excess is the price of not knowing the cost order for the pairs whose order the data do not resolve. These pairs cannot be used to exclude a system, while the standard set uses them by guessing the order. The interval construction of honestboard 0.5.1 is more conservative still, up to \NumValRobIntExcessMax\% above the standard set. On HAL the tie-robust set holds \NumHalTieRobustSet{} systems against \NumHalFrontierSet{} for the standard set. It differs from the standard set on \NumHalTieRobustBoardsLarger{} of the \NumHalBoardsPerTask{} boards with per-task costs (Table~\ref{tab:hal}). The paired cost comparison resolves the order of \NumCostSepHalMin{} to \NumCostSepHalMax{} of the pairs of systems on the \NumHalBoardsPerTask{} boards with per-task costs, against \NumCostSepSimBase{} in the base simulations and \NumCostSepSimTied{} in the nearly tied scenario (supplement S7). So HAL's boards are not in the nearly tied regime. On CORE-Bench hard the set holds all \NumCorebenchHardSystems{} systems. This is a caution: the cost order there is only partly resolved.

\paragraph{An exact set for any number of tasks.}
\label{sec:exactset}
Proposition~\ref{prop:retention} is asymptotic. A finite-sample companion for fixed costs keeps $i$ unless some rival $j$ beats it in a one-sided exact paired test at level $\alpha/m_i$. Here $m_i$ is the number of $i$'s rivals. The test is the sign test on the discordant tasks for pass/fail outcomes, and a sign-flip test for rates. The sign-flip test also needs the paired differences to be symmetric under the null. A truly undominated system has no rival that is truly better. So under the task-wise null, each rival's test rejects with probability at most $\alpha/m_i$. By Bonferroni over its rivals, the system is kept with probability at least $1-\alpha$, for any $T$. Dividing by the number of rival pairs on the whole board instead gives a joint version. It contains the whole true frontier with probability at least $1-\alpha$ for any $T$. With per-task costs, the rival sets come from the sample cost order, and a rival may truly cost more. The finite-sample guarantee then does not extend, and the exact sets are only a comparison, not a guarantee. The price is power. The exact sets are the bootstrap set's conservative cousins. Like for like, on HAL the exact per-system set holds \NumHalExactSet{} systems against \NumHalPerSystemSet{} for the per-system set of the bootstrap, and the exact joint set holds \NumHalExactJointSet{} against \NumHalFrontierSet{} for the joint set (Table~\ref{tab:resolution}).

\paragraph{What it would take to resolve a board.}
\label{sec:resolve}
A system that is in the set but off the plain frontier is one whose strongest cheaper rival leads it by $G$ standard errors, with $G\le c_\alpha$. The lead in standard-error units grows like $\sqrt T$. So with $T'$ tasks it would be $G\sqrt{T'/T}$, and the system leaves the set once $T'>T(c_\alpha/G)^2$. Section~\ref{sec:audit} reports two summaries of this for each board. The first is the task count at which half of the systems that are in the set but off the plain frontier would be excluded. The second is the task count at which a system trailing a cheaper rival by five accuracy points would be excluded, given the board's typical paired noise. Neither is a sample-size formula for a new benchmark, because the gaps on a future board are unknown. They say how far the present boards are from resolving the frontiers they draw.


\subsection{Two descriptive summaries}
\label{sec:summaries}
\paragraph{Bootstrap frontier stability.}
The bootstrap frontier stability of system $i$ is the share of task resamples in which it is on the observed frontier. Costs are resampled too when they are per task. This share describes how stable the system's membership is under the task process. It is not a probability that the system is truly undominated, and it is not calibrated. With 20 systems on 50 tasks it averages \NumValFreqUndA{} over truly undominated systems and \NumValFreqDomA{} over truly dominated ones (Table~\ref{tab:valfrontier}). We report it only as a stability measure.

\paragraph{The cheapest unresolved competitor of the best.}
\label{sec:cheapest}
The confidence set of the best \citep{hsu1981mcb} is the set of systems that we cannot rule out as the most accurate. It makes no reference to cost. It is the construction of Section~\ref{sec:set} with every other system as a rival. Its cheapest member answers a question a reader asks: which is the cheapest system the data cannot yet rule out as the best? We call this member the cheapest unresolved competitor of the best. It is not an equivalence claim. When the board is small, a system can be materially worse and still be unresolved. Not rejecting a system is not evidence that it is equal. Its coverage follows from the coverage of the set. The true best is in the set with probability at least $1-\alpha$. When it is, the cheapest member costs no more than the true best on the observed costs. For the true costs to be ordered like the observed costs, the cost order must be recoverable. The proof does not supply this. In simulation, the true best is in the set in \NumValBestInMin{} to \NumValBestInMax{} of boards over all \NumValScenarios{} scenarios. Table~\ref{tab:realism} shows nine of them. The competitor's true cost is no more than the true best's in \NumValCpbOkOtherMin{} to \NumValCpbOkOtherMax{} of boards. The exception is when true costs are nearly tied, where it is \NumValScNearTiedCostsCpbOk{} (Table~\ref{tab:realism}).

\subsection{Extensions: new entrants and selected winners}
\label{sec:extensions}
A live board changes when new submissions arrive. Also, a system reported as on the frontier was selected for being there. Two results address these facts. They build on the selective inference of the companion paper \citep{opoku2026records}. They are in the appendices, so the main text stays about the frontier as an estimated object.

\paragraph{Certifying a frontier entrant (Appendix~\ref{app:certify}).}
A new system is on the observed frontier exactly when it beats the most accurate system that is cheaper than it. So announcing an entrant is a record claim against one holder. The exact selective test of the companion paper certifies that record claim. It controls the chain-wise error over the board's history. The record claim is not a claim of Pareto membership. We prove that no certificate built on the entrant and its strongest rival alone can hold its level against the composite null that some cheaper system dominates the entrant. We give certificates of Pareto membership under a conditional null. One form uses a normal approximation. The other form is exact for pass/fail outcomes. We walked HAL's boards in submission order. \NumCertEntrants{} systems were testable entrants, meaning they beat every cheaper system on arrival. Another \NumCertCheapest{} were simply the cheapest so far, with no rival to beat. Of the entrants, \NumCertSpending{} can be certified as record claims, and \NumCertIUSpending{} as Pareto members in the normal form. Both counts use alpha-spending over each board's chain. Section S2 of the supplement gives the counts board by board. It also shows how the counts depend on how submissions are batched, and it lists every certification step.

\paragraph{The frontier winner's curse (Appendix~\ref{app:curse}).}
The selection that put a system on the frontier inflates its reported accuracy. The corrections of the companion paper apply here, with the pool of cheaper systems in place of the board. On HAL they take \NumCurseMeanShrinkPt{} points off a member's reported accuracy on average, and up to \NumCurseMaxShrinkPt{} in the largest case.

\section{Data: HAL with per-task costs rebuilt}
\label{sec:data}

For every run it hosts, HAL publishes an encrypted trace archive in its Hugging Face dataset (\path{agent-evals/hal_traces}). The dataset holds about 113~GB across \NumHalUploadsIndexed{} archives at the time of the audit. Each archive holds the run's configuration, its per-task outcomes, and its total cost as HAL computed it. It also holds the full call log. The log lists every model call the agent made, tagged with the task it served and the tokens it used. The decryption key is the public key in HAL's own harness. We read only the results member of each archive, by HTTP range requests. A results member is a few hundred kilobytes to a few hundred megabytes. We reduced each one on the fly to a compact record of outcomes, token counts and costs per task. We skipped \NumHalUploadsSkipped{} archives, because their results member was too large to decrypt in memory on our machine. The largest of these was 12.8~GB. We read the other \NumHalUploadsRead{}.

\paragraph{Per-task cost.}
A task's cost is the priced token usage of the model calls logged under it. We price the usage with HAL's own formula: fresh input tokens at the model's input price, cache reads and writes at the model's cache price, and output tokens at the output price. We use one price table for every run, which is HAL's own as read on 13 October 2026. We count each model call once. The last point matters. HAL's reported total sums the usage block of every span in the trace. An agent that calls a provider through a wrapper library logs two spans for one call: the wrapper, and the provider call inside it. Both carry the same usage block. This effect was reported on the dataset's discussion board in September 2026, and the HAL team confirmed it \citep{liu2026halissues}. HAL's pages now carry a caveat, but the totals stand. Of the \NumDupRunsTotal{} runs we read with usage, \NumDupRunsExactlyTwo{} have a reported token total of exactly twice the once-counted total. \NumDupRunsBetween{} fall between once and twice, \NumDupRunsAboveTwo{} fall above twice, and \NumDupRunsOne{} are unaffected. Over all runs, the reported totals overstate token use by \NumDupOverstatementPct\%. The doubling depends on the agent's software stack, not on the model. So it can change the cost order of agents on the same board. On the boards where it changes HAL's frontier, it moves \NumFlipCounting{} systems (Table~\ref{tab:flips}). We drop the duplicate spans. A duplicate is a call whose parent carries an identical usage block, or whose children's usage sums to its own. The supplement lists the ratio for every run. The rule leaves some structures unclear, so a different rule could remove more or fewer spans. A rule that removes some, but not all, of the spans we remove gives costs between HAL's reported totals and ours. Table~\ref{tab:flips} draws the plain frontier under both ends. No person has yet checked a stratified sample of traces against the rule by hand. As a check, we summed our per-task costs and compared them with HAL's reported total. They match to the cent on runs without nested spans that were made after the last price change of the models they used. They equal exactly half of HAL's total on runs where every provider call was wrapped. Over all \NumRecountUnnested{} kept runs with no nested spans, the recount matches HAL's total within a cent plus half a per cent on \NumRecountMatch{}. The other \NumRecountOff{} differ because HAL's total embeds the prices in force when the run was made. For example, an o3 run from May 2025 reports several times what the current table gives for the same tokens, because o3's price fell by four fifths in June 2025. An early CORE-Bench run priced cached tokens at the full input rate. A frontier compares systems with one another, so it needs one price list and one way of counting. We reprice and recount everything. We report HAL's figure beside ours in the released tables. The supplement lists every archive with HAL's total, the ratio of token totals, our recount and the run's status. The two effects compound. Over the \NumCostRatioRuns{} runs costing at least a dollar, HAL's reported totals sum to \$\NumCostReportedTotal{}, against \$\NumCostRecountedTotal{} once counted and repriced. The median run is overstated by a factor of \NumCostRatioMedian{}. The overstatement is more than half for \NumCostRatioAboveOneAndHalf{} runs, and the largest gap is a factor of \NumCostRatioMax{}.

\paragraph{Outcomes and task sets.}
Outcomes come from the run's lists of successful and failed tasks, or from its per-task evaluation results. A benchmark's task set is the tasks with an outcome in at least half of its runs. A task without a logged outcome in a run counts as a fail. This touches \NumHalSystemsMissingOutcome{} of the \NumHalRunsKept{} kept runs, and each of them misses at most 5\% of its tasks. Reruns of one configuration, and HAL's test uploads of the browser agents, are separate systems, as HAL lists them. They are each other's rivals at similar cost. Their costs are a median factor of \NumRerunCostRatioMedian{} apart (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). They add to the dominated-on-arrival count of supplement S2. On ColBench backend programming, HAL's accuracy is the share of tasks scored exactly one, and we use that. On ColBench frontend design, HAL's metric is a continuous similarity score in $[0,1]$. We keep it, so "accuracy" there is a mean score.

\paragraph{Exclusions.}
Of the \NumHalRunsRead{} run records, \NumHalRunsKept{} enter the audit. We dropped \NumHalDroppedCoverage{} runs because they carried outcomes for fewer than 95\% of the benchmark's tasks. We dropped \NumHalDroppedNoCalls{} runs because their call logs carried no model call on more than half of their tasks. An example is runs of Claude Code, which does not log through HAL's tracer. For these runs the cost is unknown, not zero. A system with an unknown cost cannot be placed on a cost-accuracy frontier, so dropping it is the only honest option. The supplement lists every dropped and skipped run. Some tasks of a kept run have an outcome but no logged model call. This happens with a logging gap, or when a run failed before its first call. We price these tasks at the run's mean cost over its costed tasks, not at zero, because a zero would push the run toward the frontier. Of the kept runs, \NumHalSystemsUncosted{} have at least one such task, and \NumHalSystemsUncostedOverTenPct{} have more than a tenth. Of all system-task pairs, \NumHalUncostedTasksPct\% are imputed. The supplement gives the count per system. On Online-Mind2Web, the browser agents' logs carry a call record per step but no token usage on any call. So no per-task cost exists for any run there, and there are no per-call usage blocks to double count. The run totals, which HAL computed from the stored run-level usage, reprice exactly under the current table. The only exception is two o3 runs, for the reason above. We use each run's repriced total, spread evenly over its tasks. So on that board, cost is a fixed number per system and is not resampled. Wherever the board appears in a table or figure, it is marked with a dagger. The dagger means that cost is a fixed number per system.

\paragraph{Why only HAL.}
Terminal-Bench 2 publishes cost for three of 244 job files, and it has no per-task results. SWE-bench Verified's cost metadata covers 47 submissions, without per-task results. Neither supports paired inference. So the audit rests on HAL's \NumHalBoards{} benchmarks. Every kept run on \NumHalBoardsPerTask{} of them has per-task outcomes and per-task costs.

\subsection{Stress tests}
\label{sec:stress}
The base design leaves out features of real boards that the guarantees depend on. Table~\ref{tab:realism} adds them one at a time to the 20-system board of 150 tasks (200 for the clustered one). Near-copy systems: six of the 20 are degraded copies of another system. Each copy turns a pass of its parent into a failure with probability 0.1, as when one agent is run with a smaller model or a lower effort. In one run the copies cost 3\% more than their parents, so a copy can only be dominated. In the other run they cost 3\% less, so a copy is a cheap, highly correlated rival that can itself belong on the frontier. Cost-outcome dependence: failed tasks cost twice as much as passed ones, as when an agent uses up many tokens on a task and then fails it. Nearly tied costs: true mean costs are a few per cent apart, closer than the standard error of a mean cost. Clustered tasks: 200 tasks in 40 groups of five. The tasks in a group share a difficulty shift and a system-by-group effect, so tasks are not independent draws. Strongly clustered tasks: 150 tasks in 15 groups of ten, with group and system-by-group effects of about 1.2 on the logit scale. So one system's outcomes within a group are strongly alike. Heavy-tailed costs: per-task costs have a coefficient of variation of 2.5 in place of 0.8. Missing cost logs: failed tasks cost twice as much as passed ones, and 30\% of the failed tasks have no logged cost. The board prices them at the system's mean over its logged tasks, as the audit does. So every system's mean cost is biased down, by a different amount for each.

\paragraph{If the standard errors are too small.}
\label{sec:inflation}
Every set above treats tasks as independent draws and one run as a system's average. Two things can make its standard errors too small. One is tasks that come in groups. The other is variation between runs that the task-level noise does not contain (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Both act on the studentised leads as one common factor $f$ on every standard error. So the set at a given $f$ can be read off the stored leads without a new bootstrap. A system stays unless its strongest cheaper rival's lead, divided by $f$, exceeds the board's margin. A design effect of $d$ is a factor $f=\sqrt d$, and the effective number of tasks is $T/f^2$. Table~\ref{tab:inflation} gives the set size at $f=1$, $1.25$, $1.5$ and $2$ for every board, and the smallest $f$ at which no system is excluded.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{Run-to-run noise.}
Each system has one run. The standard error of a single run comes from the sample variance of its task outcomes. So it already contains the independent run-to-run noise of each task. What is missing is any excess beyond that. The excess appears when a run is better or worse than its configuration's average for reasons shared across its tasks. HAL holds \NumRerunPairs{} pairs of runs of one configuration (same agent, model and settings) on \NumRerunBoards{} boards. These pairs measure the excess directly (supplement S10). The two runs of a pair differ by \NumRerunMeanAbsDiffPt{} accuracy points on average and \NumRerunMaxAbsDiffPt{} at most. In cost they differ by a median factor of \NumRerunCostRatioMedian{} (at most \NumRerunCostRatioMax{}). Suppose every task's outcome flipped independently between the two runs, at the observed rate of disagreement. Then the root-mean-square difference would be \NumRerunRmsIndepPt{} points. The observed value is \NumRerunRmsObsPt{}. This gives an excess standard deviation of \NumRerunExcessSdPt{} points per run. With \NumRerunPairs{} pairs this is weak evidence (chi-square \NumRerunChiSq{} on \NumRerunDf{} degrees of freedom, $p=\NumRerunP{}$), but it is not nothing. Also, \NumRerunPairsSharingRuns{} of the pairs share a run with another pair, which leaves at most seven degrees of freedom and $p=\NumRerunPDfSeven{}$. We carry that excess to every board through the per-task disagreement rate, and we add it to both systems of every paired comparison. An excess of that size would then grow the frontier sets from \NumRerunSetBefore{} to \NumRerunSetAfter{} systems in all. If the excess is real, the audit's sets are lower bounds. The HAL results are conditional on negligible variation shared across a run's tasks. Table~\ref{tab:inflation} shows the sets for a range of such inflation. The remedy is repeated runs, which the method accommodates by averaging them. We could not add any, because that means running the agents again.

\paragraph{Task clusters.}
We treat tasks as exchangeable draws. Where tasks cluster (several from one repository, one website, one problem family), the standard errors are optimistic, as in any task-level analysis. In simulation, with 40 groups of five tasks that share a difficulty shift and a system-by-group effect, the set built by resampling tasks contains the whole true frontier in \NumValScClusteredTasksJoint{} of boards. The set built by resampling groups does so in \NumValScClusteredTasksClusterJoint{} (Table~\ref{tab:realism}). The module can resample task groups, but most of HAL's boards offer little to resample. SWE-bench Verified mini's 50 tasks come from \NumClusterSwebenchGroups{} repositories, and USACO's 307 come from \NumClusterUsacoGroups{} difficulty divisions. So margins of \NumClusterSwebenchMarginClusters{} and \NumClusterUsacoMarginClusters{} standard errors, against \NumClusterSwebenchMarginTasks{} and \NumClusterUsacoMarginTasks{}, say more about having two or four units than about dependence. ScienceAgentBench is the one board with many groups that we could name. Its tasks come from \NumClusterSabGroups{} source repositories, and resampling them leaves the set at \NumClusterSabSetClusters{} systems, against \NumClusterSabSetTasks{} (Section~\ref{sec:audit} and supplement S15). Online-Mind2Web (tasks within websites) probably has many small groups. But the website labels are in a gated dataset, so we ran no cluster bootstrap there. CORE-Bench hard, GAIA and tau-bench airline have no groups that we could name. In the strongly clustered simulation the band covered the true curve in only \NumValScStronglyClusteredTasksBand{} of boards when tasks were resampled, and in \NumValScStronglyClusteredTasksBandCluster{} when groups were resampled. So the empirical conclusions are conditional on task-level exchangeability, as the tables of Section~\ref{sec:audit} show for a range of departures from it.

\paragraph{The rest.}
Costs depend on prices, and prices change. We fix one price table and say which one. A reader who prices a run under another table should rebuild the frontier and not reuse ours. The recount rests on HAL's published call logs, and we reran no agent. HAL's team has confirmed the double counting \citep{liu2026halissues}. It has not confirmed our recount as a whole. A request to do so is drafted in the repository. The HELM costs are recorded tokens at list prices, so they approximate a bill. They exclude the models whose reasoning tokens are not recorded. They also depend on the host chosen for the open-weight prices (Section~\ref{sec:helm}). The certificates and the winner's-curse corrections fix each system's cost at its mean. So they do not correct for selection through noisy costs. Systems may have been tuned on the public tasks. This inflates every accuracy on the board, and it is outside the model. We take outcomes as HAL recorded them. HAL has since disclosed that on tau-bench airline, the episodes of its Generalist agent that never terminated were recorded as failures. So those rows are lower bounds on that scaffold's accuracy \citep{liu2026halissues}. The guarantees of the band and the set are asymptotic. The band needs no condition on the costs. The set is proved for fixed costs, and for per-task costs whose true means are distinct. The tie-robust set removes that condition, at the price of size. On Online-Mind2Web the cost is a run total, treated as fixed. The set uses one margin for the whole board. This is conservative when the systems are many. Per-system margins would give a smaller set, with a weaker guarantee. Finally, the set is a statement about $K$ specific systems on $T$ specific tasks. A new system or a new task changes it.

\section{Recommendations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Report the frontier set, or the band, beside the frontier. A board that draws a frontier from point estimates should say which systems cannot be excluded from it, at a stated level. It should also say how many tasks that statement rests on, and how many it would take to resolve it (Table~\ref{tab:resolution}: from about a hundred to several thousand tasks on the present boards).
\item Publish per-task costs and per-call token counts. Count each model call once. Price every run under one named table, with its date. The confidence set needs per-task costs to carry the cost uncertainty. The audit shows that run totals alone can hide a difference of several times in price between two dates.
\item Where agents built on one model have nearly tied costs, use the tie-robust set. It stays valid when costs tie, and it shows the tie by growing.
\item Report the cheapest unresolved competitor of the best, with its level, beside the top system. Read it as a reason to run more tasks. Do not read it as a purchasing recommendation.
\item Certify frontier entrants with an error budget that is spent over the calendar. Say which claim is certified: the record claim against the most accurate cheaper system (exact), or Pareto membership (the composite test, under its conditional null). Fix costs on arrival, because the guarantee needs it (Appendix~\ref{app:certify}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
A cost-accuracy frontier is an estimate. If we treat it as one, with a confidence set in place of a line, HAL's benchmarks say less than the lines suggest, and more than nothing. On some boards the frontier is sharp. On most it is wide. On a few it says nothing until more tasks are run. The tools here let a maintainer say which case a board is in. They hold for tasks drawn from the benchmark's task process, and they are conditional on the systems submitted. They say nothing about tuning on public tasks or about new kinds of tasks. The audit also takes tasks to be independent and one run to be a system's average. If the true standard errors are 1.5 times ours, the HAL standard sets grow from \NumInflHalSetOne{} to \NumInflHalSetOneFive{} of \NumInflHalSystems{} systems (Table~\ref{tab:inflation}). If the extra variation behind this is real, the sets reported here are lower bounds.

\section{Certifying a frontier entrant}
\label{app:certify}
This appendix gives the certificates for a new frontier entrant and the proofs that go with them. HAL's own timeline is in supplement S2.

\subsection{Ingredients from the companion paper}
\label{sec:ingredients}
We use five pieces of \citet{opoku2026records} below. Each is stated here in the form we need.

\paragraph{The selective exact $p$-value.} For a holder $a$ and a challenger $b$ on the same $T$ tasks with pass/fail outcomes, let $n_b$ be the number of tasks $b$ passes and $a$ fails, $n_a$ the reverse, and $n=n_a+n_b$. A leaderboard tests $b$ only because $\bar y_b>\bar y_a$. That is, $n_b>n_a$. The selective exact $p$-value is the tail of the sign test given that event, $p_{\mathrm{sel}}=\Pr(X\ge n_b\mid X>n/2)$ with $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,1/2)$. For outcomes that are rates in $[0,1]$, we apply the same conditioning to a sign-flip test of the paired differences. We run that test by Monte Carlo over 200{,}000 sign patterns.

\paragraph{The conditional task-wise dominance null.} This is the null under which $p_{\mathrm{sel}}$ is valid. On every task, given the two systems' outcomes on the other tasks, $b$ is no more likely to pass than $a$. Tasks are independent. It is the natural "$b$ is not better than $a$" for paired data. Proposition~1 of the companion paper shows that under this null $p_{\mathrm{sel}}$ is super-uniform given the record event. This holds as long as the holder was chosen by an event that is increasing in the holder's own outcomes and does not involve $b$.

\paragraph{The per-date factor and the best-first order.} Submissions that share a date are ordered best first. So the challenger tested on a date is the best of that date. This is a second selection, and the record event does not cover it. We multiply the $p$-value by $g$, the number of submissions on the challenger's date. This is a Bonferroni factor over the date's members, and it restores the guarantee.

\paragraph{Alpha-spending.} The $t$-th tested step is tested at level $\alpha_t=6\alpha/(\pi^2t^2)$. The levels sum to $\alpha$. So by the union bound, the probability of certifying any null step over the whole chain is at most $\alpha$. This holds for any number of steps and any dependence. It needs two things. Each step's $p$-value must be valid given that the step occurred. And the step's level must be settled before its outcomes are looked at.

\paragraph{The online fallback.} The $t$-th step is tested at $\alpha_t$ plus the level of the previous step, when that step was certified \citep{wiens2003fallback,tian2021onlinefwer}. So a run of certified steps builds up budget. The family-wise guarantee is the same. The proof is a disjointness argument over the runs of certified steps that feed each null step.

\subsection{The certificates}
\label{sec:certify}
A live board changes when submissions arrive. A new system $b$ lands on the frontier when it beats every system cheaper than it. The maintainer who then tests it has selected it for exactly that reason, and a test that ignores the selection is biased. The frontier event looks harder than the record event, because it is an intersection of events against several systems. As an event, it is not harder.

Let $C(b)=\{j\ \text{earlier}: c_j\le c_b\}$ be the cheaper-or-equal systems when $b$ arrives. Let $j^*$ be the most accurate member of $C(b)$, with ties broken by a rule that does not look at $b$. Then
\[
b\ \text{is on the observed frontier on arrival}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bar y_b>\bar y_{j^*},
\]
because beating the most accurate cheaper system is the same as beating every cheaper system. A tie with a strictly cheaper system leaves $b$ dominated, and a tie at equal cost leaves both on the frontier. Also, $b$ counts as an entrant only when the inequality is strict. So the frontier event is the record event of the companion paper for the single pair (holder $j^*$, challenger $b$). The holder was chosen by an event, "$j^*$ is the most accurate system in $C(b)$". This event is increasing in $j^*$'s own outcomes task by task. Otherwise it depends only on the other cheaper systems, and it does not involve $b$. These are the conditions of Proposition~1 of that paper.

\begin{proposition}[frontier entrant]
\label{prop:entrant}
Fix the costs before the outcomes are seen. Under the conditional task-wise dominance null for the pair $(j^*,b)$ and independence across tasks, the selective exact $p$-value $p_{\mathrm{sel}}(b\ \text{vs}\ j^*)$ is super-uniform conditional on the event that $b$ is on the observed frontier on arrival. A rejection at level $\alpha$ certifies that $b$ beats the system that was the most accurate cheaper system when $b$ arrived.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[what the certificate says, and what it does not]
\label{rem:certificate}
The null tested is pairwise. Suppose some cheaper system $k$ truly dominates $b$, while the observed best cheaper system $j^*$ is a weak relative of $b$. Such a relative is the same agent with a smaller model, a lower effort setting, or a rerun. Its outcomes are correlated with $b$'s. Then $b$ can beat $j^*$ with the stated guarantee and still be truly dominated by $k$. The test is not of level $\alpha$ for the composite null "some cheaper system dominates $b$". In simulation, the apparent rival is such a near-copy, and a truly dominating independent system is in the pool. Then a dominated entrant is certified in \NumEntrantNullCopyMin{} to \NumEntrantNullCopyMax\% of the cases in which it lands on the frontier. When every cheaper system is independent of it, the rate is \NumEntrantNullIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIndepMax\% (\NumEntrantNullR{} boards per case). So the certificate is a record claim restricted to the cheaper pool. It becomes a certificate of Pareto membership only under an added assumption. The assumption is this: whenever $b$ is truly dominated by a cheaper system, it is also task-wise dominated by the observed best cheaper system. One could test $b$ against each cheaper system with the pairwise selective test and require every test to reject. This does not repair the problem, because each pairwise test conditions on its own event and not on the whole frontier event. Conditioning every test on the whole event does repair it, at the price of a normal approximation. That is the next proposition.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Pareto certificate]
\label{prop:pareto}
Let the vector of accuracies be approximately normal, $\bar y\sim N(\theta,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma$ the task-level covariance divided by $T$, and let $E=\{\bar y_b\ge\bar y_k\ \text{for every}\ k\in C(b)\}$ be the frontier event. For each $k\in C(b)$ let $p_k$ be the polyhedral selective $p$-value of \citet{lee2016exact} for $H_k:\theta_b\le\theta_k$ conditional on $E$, with the lattice correction of the companion paper. Then $p_{\mathrm{IU}}=\max_{k\in C(b)}p_k$ is super-uniform, conditional on $E$, under the composite null "some system in $C(b)$ truly dominates $b$". Rejecting when $p_{\mathrm{IU}}\le\alpha$ certifies at level $\alpha$ that no cheaper-or-equal system is truly at least as accurate as $b$. That is, $b$ is on the true frontier of its pool.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Under the composite null some $H_k$ holds. $E$ is a polyhedron in $\bar y$, so by the polyhedral lemma $p_k$ is uniform conditional on $E$ when $\theta_b=\theta_k$ and super-uniform when $\theta_b<\theta_k$. So $\Pr(p_{\mathrm{IU}}\le\alpha\mid E)\le\Pr(p_k\le\alpha\mid E)\le\alpha$. An intersection-union test needs no correction for the number of rivals \citep{berger1982iu}.
\end{proof}
Two things sit outside the proof. First, the covariance is a plug-in estimate. Second, the lattice correction is a continuity adjustment for accuracies that live on the grid $1/T$. It relaxes each constraint by half a step, and it takes the tail from half a step below the observed value. One of these two actions is conservative, and the other is not. The null rows of Table~\ref{tab:entrantnull}, at most \NumEntrantNullIUCopyMax\% and \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMax\%, are the check on both.
The two certificates answer different questions and have different prices. The pairwise test is exact for any $T$. It asks whether $b$ beat the system it displaced. The composite test is exact only under normality with a plug-in covariance. It asks whether $b$ beats every cheaper system at once, and it conditions on an event that truncates the statistic more. Table~\ref{tab:entrantnull} shows both tests on the three simulated worlds. With a near-copy rival, the pairwise test certifies a truly dominated entrant in \NumEntrantNullCopyMin{} to \NumEntrantNullCopyMax\% of the cases in which it lands on the frontier. The composite test does so in \NumEntrantNullIUCopyMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIUCopyMax\%. With independent rivals, the pairwise test certifies \NumEntrantNullIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIndepMax\% and the composite test \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMax\%. When the entrant is truly better than every cheaper system by half a logit, the pairwise test certifies it \NumEntrantPowerMin{} to \NumEntrantPowerMax\% of the time and the composite test \NumEntrantPowerIUMin{} to \NumEntrantPowerIUMax\%. The composite test gives up nothing in power for its stronger claim. The exact sign test is conservative because its statistic is discrete. This shows in its null rates (\NumEntrantNullIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIndepMax\% against the composite test's \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMax\%). The normal statistic has no such step. What the composite test gives up is exactness. The audit reports both.

\begin{proposition}[exact Pareto certificate]
\label{prop:exact}
Fix the costs. Outcomes are pass/fail. For $k\in C(b)$ let $\mathcal G_k$ be the information in the unordered pairs $\{y_{bt},y_{kt}\}$ and in the outcomes of all other systems. Let $H_k$ be the statement that tasks are independent and, on every task with $y_{bt}\ne y_{kt}$, $\Pr(y_{bt}=1\mid\mathcal G_k)\le\tfrac12$. Equality is the sharp null that $b$ and $k$ are exchangeable given the others. Let $D_k$ be the number of tasks on which $b$ and $k$ differ, $N_k$ the number $b$ wins, $n_{11}$ the number both pass, and
\[
n_{0k}=\max\Bigl\{\bigl\lceil D_k/2\bigr\rceil,\ \bigl\lceil T\max\nolimits_{j\in C(b)\setminus k}\bar y_j-n_{11}\bigr\rceil\Bigr\},\qquad
p_k=\frac{\Pr(X\ge N_k)}{\Pr(X\ge n_{0k})},\quad X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12).
\]
On the frontier event $E=\{\bar y_b\ge\bar y_j\ \text{for every}\ j\in C(b)\}$, two things hold. (i) Under $H_k$, $\Pr(p_k\le u\mid\mathcal G_k,E)\le u$ for every $u$, with equality at the attainable values under the sharp null. (ii) If $H_k$ holds for some $k\in C(b)$, then $\Pr(\max_k p_k\le\alpha\mid E)\le\alpha$, for any number of tasks. For outcomes in $[0,1]$ that are not pass/fail, the same construction by random swaps is valid under the sharp null only.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given $\mathcal G_k$, the unordered pair on each discordant task is fixed. So $N_k$ is a sum of $D_k$ independent indicators with success probabilities $q_t\le\tfrac12$ under $H_k$, and it is $\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12)$ under the sharp null. Swapping $b$'s and $k$'s outcomes on a set of tasks leaves $\bar y_b+\bar y_k$ unchanged. It also leaves every $\bar y_j$ with $j\notin\{b,k\}$ unchanged. So given $\mathcal G_k$, the event $E$ is the event $\{N_k\ge n_{0k}\}$, with $n_{0k}$ a function of $\mathcal G_k$. The first term of the maximum comes from $\bar y_b\ge\bar y_k$. The second comes from $\bar y_b\ge\bar y_j$ for the other systems, and it is omitted when $C(b)=\{k\}$. Under the sharp null, the law of $N_k$ given $\mathcal G_k$ and $E$ is $\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12)$ truncated to $\{N\ge n_{0k}\}$. And $p_k$ is the tail probability of that law at the observed value, so it is exactly uniform on its attainable values. Under $H_k$, the probability mass function of $N_k$ relative to that of $\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12)$ is proportional to $e_n(r)/\binom{D_k}{n}$, with $e_n$ the elementary symmetric polynomial and $r_t=q_t/(1-q_t)\le1$. Let $S_n=e_n(r)/\binom{D_k}{n}$. Maclaurin's inequality gives $S_{n+1}^{1/(n+1)}\le S_n^{1/n}$. So $S_{n+1}\le S_n\cdot S_n^{1/n}\le S_n$, because $S_n\le1$ for $r_t\le1$. The likelihood ratio is therefore nonincreasing. So $N_k$ is smaller than $\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12)$ in the likelihood-ratio order, and truncation to $\{N\ge n_{0k}\}$ preserves this order. So $\Pr(p_k\le u\mid\mathcal G_k,E)\le u$, which is (i). For (ii), $E$ is the same event for every $k$. If $H_{k_0}$ holds, then $\{\max_kp_k\le\alpha\}\subseteq\{p_{k_0}\le\alpha\}$. Averaging (i) over $\mathcal G_{k_0}$ given $E$ gives the bound. An intersection-union test needs no correction for the number of rivals \citep{berger1982iu}.
\end{proof}
The $p$-value is the sign test of the companion paper with a higher truncation point, $n_{0k}\ge\lceil D_k/2\rceil$. It needs no normality, covariance estimate, lattice correction or Monte Carlo. It needs only pass/fail outcomes, and that the null be read as conditional on the other systems' outcomes. That last point is the price. A system that truly dominates $b$ in means need not satisfy $H_k$. In the near-copy world, $b$ wins every discordant task against the independent dominating system on which the copy passes, so $\Pr(y_{bt}=1\mid\mathcal G_k)=1$ there. The proof does not cover that world. Table~\ref{tab:entrantnull} is the evidence for this. In the audit's three simulated worlds, the exact certificate certifies a dominated entrant \NumEntrantNullExCopyMin{} to \NumEntrantNullExCopyMax\% of the time with a near-copy rival and \NumEntrantNullExIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullExIndepMax\% with independent ones. It certifies a truly better entrant \NumEntrantPowerExMin{} to \NumEntrantPowerExMax\% of the time. These rates are on \NumEntrantNullRExact{} boards per row. The guarantee is conditional on the weak event $E$ ($\ge$). Certification also requires $\bar y_b>\bar y_j$ for every $j$, which can only reduce the probability of certification.

\begin{proposition}[no pairwise certificate holds its level]
\label{prop:impossible}
Let $\phi_T$ be a test of the composite null that $b$ is dominated by some cheaper system. The test uses the data only through the outcomes of the entrant $b$ and of the observed most accurate cheaper system $j^*$. Suppose there is $\varepsilon(m)\to0$ such that $\phi_T$ rejects with probability at least $1-\varepsilon(m)$ whenever $b$ wins every task on which $b$ and $j^*$ differ and there are at least $m$ such tasks. Then there are configurations with $\mu_b\in(0,1)$ that have three properties. In them, $b$ is truly dominated by a strictly cheaper system. The frontier event has probability tending to $\tfrac12$. And the conditional rejection probability given the frontier event tends to one.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put in $C(b)$ two systems. One is a strictly cheaper system $k$ with $\mu_k=\mu_b$, independent of $b$. The other is a cheaper copy $j$ of $b$ whose passes are flipped to failures independently with probability $\delta_T=T^{-3/4}$. Then $k$ dominates $b$. Let $F\approx\mu_b\delta_TT=\mu_bT^{1/4}\to\infty$ be the number of flipped passes. Then $\bar y_b>\bar y_j$ with probability tending to one, and $\bar y_b>\bar y_k$ with probability tending to $\tfrac12$. So $\Pr(E)\to\tfrac12$. Given $E$, $j^*=j$ exactly when $\bar y_b-\bar y_k\ge F/T$. The left side is of order $T^{-1/2}$, with a continuous positive limit density at $0^+$, while $F/T$ is of order $T^{-3/4}$. So $\Pr(j^*=j\mid E)\to1$. Against $j$, $b$ wins all $F$ discordant tasks and loses none, and $F\to\infty$ in probability. For each fixed $m$, $\liminf_T\Pr(\phi_T\ \text{rejects}\mid E)\ge1-\varepsilon(m)$, because given $E$ both $F\ge m$ and $j^*=j$ hold with probability tending to one. Letting $m\to\infty$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}
The class is not vacuous. Any test that rejects once $b$ has won a fixed number of discordant tasks and lost none belongs to it, and the selective sign test is one. A test that needs on the order of $\sqrt T$ wins before it rejects lies outside it. That is the number an independent rival's sampling noise produces. Such a test pays for its protection by failing to certify a genuine entrant who beats $j^*$ by less than that. The convergence is so slow, of order $T^{-1/4}$, that the proposition describes the shape of the problem and not its size. Table~\ref{tab:entrantnull} gives the size at 50 to 300 tasks. A leaderboard that lists an agent twice holds a pair of this kind in a weaker form. The two entries may use a stronger and a weaker model, or two reasoning-effort settings. HAL's own reruns of one configuration agree on \NumRerunAgreeMin{} to \NumRerunAgreeMax\% of tasks. That is far from the asymptotic construction, which needs agreement on all but about $T^{1/4}$ tasks. The simulations show what pairs that close do at realistic sizes. So any certificate of Pareto membership must look at the whole cheaper pool. That is what Propositions~\ref{prop:pareto} and~\ref{prop:exact} do.

\begin{corollary}[a chain of entrants]
\label{cor:chain}
Let the step index $t$ count the dates on which at least one entrant arrived. Test every entrant of the $t$-th such date at level $\alpha_t$ (alpha-spending), or at $\alpha_t$ plus the previous such date's level when every entrant of that date was certified (fallback). Multiply its $p$-value by its per-date factor $g$. Then the family-wise error of the chain of certificates is at most $\alpha$. This holds in the sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:entrant} when the pairwise $p$-values are used, and in the sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:pareto} when the composite ones are.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A date's level is a function of which earlier dates had entrants and, for the fallback, of their outcomes. It is a function of no outcome of the date itself. So it is predictable, and the null of each proposition is read conditionally on all earlier systems' outcomes. For a null submission $s$ on date $d$, $\Pr(s\ \text{certified})=\Pr(E_s)\Pr(p_s\le\alpha_t/g\mid E_s)\le\Pr(E_s)\,\alpha_t/g$, by Proposition~\ref{prop:entrant} or~\ref{prop:pareto}. Sum over the at most $g$ submissions of the date. The expected number of certified null entrants on the date, given the past, is then at most $\alpha_t\Pr(\text{an entrant arrives}\mid\text{past})\le\alpha_t$. The step index $t_d$ is predictable and takes distinct values on distinct dates with entrants. So the expected number of certified null entrants over the whole chain is at most $\sum_t\alpha_t=\alpha$. For the fallback, consider the event that date $t$ is the first date on which a null entrant is certified. Every earlier date whose level was passed along the run that feeds $t$ had all its entrants certified, and none of them null. So the run consists of non-null dates. The runs that feed different first-failure dates are disjoint. The levels available to null dates sum to at most $\alpha$, as in Corollary~2 of \citet{opoku2026records}. Passing a date's level on when only some of its entrants were certified would break this. A date holding a certified non-null entrant and an uncertified null one would sit inside the run and have its level counted twice.
\end{proof}
Indexing each same-date entrant by its own step would make an entrant's level depend on its rank within the date. That rank is settled only after the date's results are seen. Counting dates avoids this and spends one level per date.

\begin{remark}[random costs]
When costs are measured per task, $C(b)$ is itself random. Also, $b$'s mean cost comes from the same run as its outcomes, because tokens are spent on failed tasks too. So the holder is not strictly free of $b$. For certification, we fix each system's cost at its mean on the day of its arrival. We check the consequence by resampling every system's per-task costs and rebuilding the chain. On the boards with per-task costs, an entrant keeps the same holder in \NumCertJitterStableMin{} to \NumCertJitterStableMax{} of \NumCertJitterR{} resamples. The number of entrants moves within \NumCertJitterEntrantsMin{} to \NumCertJitterEntrantsMax{} around \NumCertJitterEntrantsBase{}. The frontier set of Section~\ref{sec:set} is where the cost uncertainty is carried in full.
\end{remark}

\section{The frontier winner's curse}
\label{app:curse}
A system reported as "on the frontier" was selected because it beat every cheaper system. Its reported accuracy is inflated by this selection, like a leaderboard winner's. The selection event, $\bar y_b\ge\bar y_j$ for all $j\in C(b)$, is a polyhedron in the vector of accuracies. So the conditional method and the iterated bootstrap of \citet{opoku2026records} apply, with the pool $C(b)\cup\{b\}$ in place of the whole board. The conditional estimate is the median-unbiased estimate under the truncated normal law of $\bar y_b$, given that it is the largest of its pool \citep{lee2016exact,andrews2024winners}. The bootstrap finds the winner of the pool again in every task resample. It takes the winner's mean excess over its own observed accuracy as the bias. A second bootstrap level removes the bias that the first correction leaves. Costs are fixed at their means, as in certification. A system with no cheaper rival, the cheapest on the board, has a pool of one and nothing to correct.
