\begin{abstract}
A leaderboard that reports cost beside accuracy is read through its Pareto frontier, the systems that no cheaper system beats. The frontier is drawn from point estimates on a sample of tasks, so it is an estimate, and no leaderboard reports how uncertain it is. We give a simultaneous confidence band for the frontier curve and a confidence set for its members that contains the whole true frontier with probability at least $1-\alpha$. The band is proved for fixed or per-task costs. The set is proved for fixed costs, and for per-task costs when the true mean costs are distinct; a tie-robust set, a step-down on paired cost and accuracy comparisons, drops that condition at the price of a larger set. We check both on known truth in \NumValScenarios{} scenarios and report where they fail, including nearly tied costs and cheaper near-copies of frontier systems. We rebuild per-task costs for \NumHalSystems{} runs on \NumHalBoards{} benchmarks of the Holistic Agent Leaderboard (HAL) from its public call logs. HAL's cost totals double count nested trace spans on \NumDupRunsAffected{} of \NumDupRunsTotal{} runs. Counting each call once changes HAL's frontier on \NumFlipBoardsChangedCounting{} benchmarks, and counting and pricing together change it on \NumFlipBoardsChanged{} of \NumFlipBoards{}. With uncertainty added, the share of systems that cannot be excluded from the frontier runs from \NumHalSetShareMinPct\% to \NumHalSetShareMaxPct\% across HAL boards, and from \NumHelmSetShareMinPct\% to \NumHelmSetShareMaxPct\% on a second leaderboard, HELM Capabilities, whose boards have \NumHelmTasksMin{} to \NumHelmTasksMax{} tasks. Certified frontier entrants keep a positive lead on fresh tasks in \NumReplHalCertPos{} of cases on HAL and \NumReplHelmCertPos{} on HELM, though from few distinct entrant-holder pairs. The certificates and a winner's-curse correction for frontier members are in the appendices, each stated under its own null. The methods are in the open-source package \texttt{honestboard}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Leaderboards for AI agents increasingly report what a run costs beside how often it succeeds. The Holistic Agent Leaderboard (HAL) \citep{kapoor2025hal} does this for every benchmark it hosts, and it draws the cost-accuracy picture the way an economist would: a system is worth looking at only if no cheaper system does at least as well. The systems that pass this test form the Pareto frontier, and HAL's own write-up stresses how few systems sit on it. Readers and the teams that build agents act on that picture. A system on the frontier is a reasonable one to consider at its price, and a system below it usually is not.

The picture is drawn from point estimates. Each system's accuracy is its success rate on a sample of tasks: \NumAssistantbenchTasks{} on AssistantBench, \NumCorebenchHardTasks{} on CORE-Bench hard, \NumTaubenchAirlineTasks{} on tau-bench airline and on the SWE-bench Verified mini split. With samples that small the rate carries a standard error of several points, and the frontier, which is a function of those rates, moves when the rates move. A system just below the frontier might sit on it under another draw of tasks, and a system on it might not. HAL reports no uncertainty for its frontiers.

This paper supplies that uncertainty. It answers four questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item How high can the frontier curve, the best accuracy available at each cost, really be, and how low; and which systems cannot be excluded from the frontier at a stated confidence level?
\item How wrong is the plain frontier when the truth is known, and where do the guarantees fail?
\item What is the cheapest system a reader cannot yet rule out as the best?
\item How many tasks would a board need before its frontier is resolved?
\end{enumerate}
It also audits HAL with per-task costs rebuilt from its call logs, and it gives, in two appendices, certificates for new frontier entrants and a winner's-curse correction for frontier members.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A simultaneous confidence band for the frontier curve itself, valid with fixed or per-task costs and with no condition on ties among the true mean costs (Section~\ref{sec:band}).
\item A confidence set for the frontier's members that contains the whole true frontier with probability at least $1-\alpha$, proved for fixed costs and for per-task costs without ties; a tie-robust version that drops the condition on ties; and exact finite-sample sets (Section~\ref{sec:set}).
\item Resolution numbers, the task count at which a board would start to resolve its own frontier, and two descriptive summaries, a bootstrap frontier stability and the cheapest unresolved competitor of the best (Sections~\ref{sec:resolve} and~\ref{sec:summaries}).
\item A known-truth validation in \NumValScenarios{} scenarios, including near-copy systems, outcome-dependent costs, nearly tied costs and clustered tasks, a stability check on held-out halves of real boards, and an account of where the guarantees fail (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).
\item An audit of HAL with consistent costing: each model call counted once, one price table, the double count quantified for every run, the effects of counting and of pricing on HAL's published frontiers reported separately, and the sensitivity of the audit to the costing rules (Sections~\ref{sec:data} and~\ref{sec:audit}).
\item A second leaderboard, HELM Capabilities, audited with the same tools, and a fresh-task replication of the entrant certificates on both leaderboards (Section~\ref{sec:helm}).
\item In the appendices, two certificates for a new frontier entrant with chain-wise error control, one a record claim and one a certificate of Pareto membership under a stated null, a proof that no certificate built on the entrant's strongest rival alone can hold its level, and the winner's-curse correction for frontier members.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What the paper does not claim.}
The tools are adapted, not invented from nothing. The set is Hsu's multiple-comparisons-with-the-best construction with each system's rivals restricted to the systems that cost no more and the margin taken jointly, the exact sets are paired sign tests with Bonferroni, and the certificates are the selective tests of a companion paper with a different conditioning event. The inference is conditional on the submitted systems and takes the tasks as draws from the benchmark's task process. It does not account for tuning on public tasks or for which systems were submitted (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).

\paragraph{Related work.}
Uncertainty in leaderboard rankings has been treated through confidence sets for ranks \citep{mogstad2024ranks,neuhof2026rankintervals,neuhof2026ranking}, confidence sets of the best \citep{hsu1981mcb,hansen2011mcs}, the winner's curse of a selected system \citep{andrews2024winners,bakshi2026winners}, and standard errors for language-model evaluations \citep{miller2024errorbars,bowyer2025clt,madaan2024variance}. The observed frontier is a free-disposal-hull estimator, and inference for such frontiers is developed in econometrics for sampled firms with exactly measured inputs and outputs: the asymptotics of the free-disposal-hull estimator \citep{park2000fdh} and the bootstrap for nonparametric frontier models \citep{simar1998bootstrap,simar2000state}. Tests of stochastic dominance \citep{linton2005sd} ask a different question. The sampling error of an efficient frontier is studied in finance \citep{brittenjones1999} and that of Pareto fronts under noisy objectives in evolutionary optimisation \citep{fieldsend2005noisy}. Here the units are systems whose coordinates, accuracy and cost, are both estimated from the same tasks, and the tasks are shared across units. Identifying a Pareto set from noisy observations has also been studied where the analyst chooses what to sample next: Pareto-set identification with $(\epsilon,\delta)$ guarantees from bandit feedback \citep{auer2016pareto}, active learning of the Pareto front \citep{zuluaga2013pal}, and multi-objective ranking and selection in simulation \citep{applegate2020mors}. Cost has entered leaderboard design through HAL \citep{kapoor2025hal}, the argument that agent evaluations should report it \citep{kapoor2024agents}, and the cost-of-pass framework \citep{erol2025costofpass}, all from point estimates. To our knowledge no earlier work gives a published cost-accuracy frontier a simultaneous confidence band and a confidence set for its members, though the literatures above are close, and a systematic comparison with them is beyond one paper.

\paragraph{Findings.}
HAL's cost totals double count nested trace spans on \NumDupRunsAffected{} of \NumDupRunsTotal{} runs and carry the prices of the month each run was made. Counting each call once changes the point-estimate frontier on \NumFlipBoardsChangedCounting{} benchmarks; counting and pricing together change it on \NumFlipBoardsChanged{} of \NumFlipBoards{}, with \NumFlipLost{} of the \NumFlipFrontierReported{} systems on the frontiers drawn from HAL's reported totals leaving and \NumFlipGained{} others joining, \NumFlipSystemsChanged{} changes in all, \NumFlipPricing{} of those changes through the pricing and \NumFlipCounting{} through the counting (Section~\ref{sec:audit}). With uncertainty added, \NumHalObservedFrontier{} systems sit on the observed frontiers, but the share that cannot be excluded runs from \NumHalSetShareMinPct\% on \NumHalSetShareMinName{} to \NumHalSetShareMaxPct\% on \NumHalSetShareMaxNames{}, and tends to fall as the task count grows, though not monotonically. In the base simulations the set keeps the whole true frontier in \NumValBaseJointMin{} to \NumValBaseJointMax{} of boards against the nominal 0.95. With nearly tied true costs the standard set keeps it in only \NumValScNearTiedCostsJoint{}, and with cheaper near-copies of frontier systems in \NumValScCheaperNearCopiesJoint{}. The tie-robust set keeps it in \NumValScNearTiedCostsRobJoint{} and \NumValScCheaperNearCopiesRobJoint{} of boards, in the nearly tied case only by returning the whole board, because the data cannot resolve such a frontier (Sections~\ref{sec:validation} and~\ref{sec:ties}). On a second leaderboard, HELM Capabilities, the share that cannot be excluded runs from \NumHelmSetShareMinPct\% to \NumHelmSetShareMaxPct\%, and entrants whose record claim is certified keep a positive lead on fresh tasks in \NumReplHalCertPos{} of cases on HAL and \NumReplHelmCertPos{} on HELM (Section~\ref{sec:helm}).

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

A board has $K$ systems evaluated on the same $T$ tasks. System $k$ scores $y_{kt}\in[0,1]$ on task $t$: a pass or fail on most benchmarks, a partial score on some. Its accuracy is the mean $\bar y_k=T^{-1}\sum_t y_{kt}$, which estimates $\mu_k=\E\,y_{kt}$. Its cost is either one number per system, $c_k$ (the price of the run divided by $T$), or one number per task, $c_{kt}$, with $\bar c_k$ the mean estimating $\gamma_k=\E\,c_{kt}$; on HAL the per-task costs come from the call logs (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

System $j$ \emph{dominates} system $i$ when $c_j\le c_i$ and $\bar y_j\ge\bar y_i$ with at least one of the two strict. The \emph{observed frontier} is the set of systems that no other system dominates. Two systems equal in both cost and accuracy dominate neither the other, so both can be on the frontier; this is the only tie convention used, and it is applied throughout. The \emph{true frontier} is the same set with $\mu_k$ and $\gamma_k$ in place of $\bar y_k$ and $\bar c_k$: a system is truly undominated when no other system costs no more and is at least as accurate with one of the two strict.

\paragraph{What is estimated.}
The systems are taken as given. The inference is conditional on the $K$ submitted systems and says nothing about systems that were tried and not submitted, or tuned on the public tasks. The tasks are draws from the benchmark's task process, independent across tasks and dependent across systems on the same task (hard tasks are hard for everyone). The target is each system's expected accuracy $\mu_k$ on new tasks from that process. For a fixed set of tasks the empirical frontier is exactly determined, and the uncertainty described here arises only because the tasks are a sample. The target is a benchmark population, not a deployment population: a result about new kinds of tasks, other repositories or sites, or real use is not covered, and clustered tasks are where it is most at risk (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Cost is the priced token usage of reproducing the run, in dollars per task under one price table, and its target is the expected value $\gamma_k$ under the task process. A frontier therefore says which systems are cheapest to reproduce at a given accuracy at the prices of the table, not which were the best buys when they were run. The price table is a fixed list, and a system's cost is the priced token count of its run, which varies with the tasks drawn, so costs are random where prices are not. Three things are not modelled: tuning of a system on the public tasks, run-to-run variation of a system beyond independent task-level noise, and changes in prices over time.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The observed frontier and why it is not enough}
The observed frontier is what every cost-aware leaderboard draws. It is a function of $K$ estimated accuracies, each with a standard error of order $T^{-1/2}$, and it is a discontinuous function: moving one accuracy by a hair can add or remove a system. Section~\ref{sec:validation} measures how often it is wrong when the truth is known. The short version is that with \NumValR{} simulated boards of 20 systems on 50 tasks, \NumValNaiveFalseA\% of the systems on the observed frontier are truly dominated and \NumValNaiveMissedA\% of the true frontier is missed.

\subsection{A confidence band for the frontier curve}
\label{sec:band}
What a cost-aware leaderboard draws is a curve: at each cost $x$, the best accuracy available from any system whose cost is at most $x$,
\[
A(x)=\max_{j:\,\gamma_j\le x}\mu_j,
\]
with $\gamma_j$ the true mean cost of system $j$: a staircase that steps up at the cost of each frontier member. The plain frontier is this curve drawn with the sample means $\bar y_j$ and $\bar c_j$ in place of $\mu_j$ and $\gamma_j$. A simultaneous band for $A$ follows from a simultaneous band for all $2K$ means at once. Let $\se_j$ and $\mathrm{sg}_j$ be the standard errors of $\bar y_j$ and $\bar c_j$ (zero for a cost that is a fixed number), and let $c_{\mathrm{band}}$ be the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile, over task resamples that carry outcomes and costs together, of the largest of $|\bar y_j^{(b)}-\bar y_j|/\se_j$ and $|\bar c_j^{(b)}-\bar c_j|/\mathrm{sg}_j$ over all systems. Each system then has an accuracy interval $[\bar y_j\mp c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j]$ and a cost interval $[\bar c_j\mp c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j]$, and
\[
L(x)=\max_{j:\ \bar c_j+c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j\le x}(\bar y_j-c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j),\qquad
U(x)=\max_{j:\ \bar c_j-c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j\le x}(\bar y_j+c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j).
\]
A system enters the lower edge only when its cost is surely within the budget $x$ and the upper edge when it possibly is, so neither edge relies on having the cost order right.
\begin{proposition}[band]
\label{prop:band}
Let the task vectors of outcomes and costs be i.i.d., with every random accuracy and cost having positive variance, let $K$ be fixed and each true accuracy $\mu_j$ fixed in $(0,1)$ as $T\to\infty$. Then
\[
\Pr\bigl(L(x)\le A(x)\le U(x)\ \text{for every}\ x\ge\min_j\gamma_j\bigr)\ \ge\ 1-\alpha-o(1),
\]
with costs fixed or measured per task, and with no condition on ties among the true mean costs. ($A(x)$ is a maximum over an empty set below the cheapest true cost.)
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $E$ be the event that every $\mu_j$ lies in its accuracy interval and every $\gamma_j$ in its cost interval. If $\bar c_j+c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j\le x$ then on $E$ $\gamma_j\le x$, so $\bar y_j-c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j\le\mu_j\le A(x)$; hence $L(x)\le A(x)$. If $\gamma_j\le x$ then on $E$ $\bar c_j-c_{\mathrm{band}}\mathrm{sg}_j\le\gamma_j\le x$, so the maximizer $j$ of $\mu_j$ over $\{\gamma_j\le x\}$ enters $U(x)$ with $\bar y_j+c_{\mathrm{band}}\se_j\ge\mu_j=A(x)$; hence $U(x)\ge A(x)$. Both hold for every $x$ on $E$. By the multivariate central limit theorem and the consistency of the bootstrap for the mean vector \citep{bickel1981bootstrap}, with the standard errors converging in probability by Slutsky's theorem, the bootstrap law of the maximal studentised deviation converges to that of the maximum of the absolute values of a Gaussian vector whose coordinates have positive variance, whose distribution function is continuous and strictly increasing, so $\Pr(E)\ge1-\alpha-o(1)$.
\end{proof}
The proposition is asymptotic with each $\mu_j$ fixed in $(0,1)$, and it is the systems with few passes or few failures that it does not protect. A bootstrap-$t$ interval undercovers for them and collapses to a point when a system passes or fails every task in the sample; a system with true accuracy $0.02$ on $50$ tasks fails every task a third of the time, and on a small board of four systems the band built from bootstrap-$t$ intervals alone covered the true curve only $62\%$ of the time. For pass/fail outcomes a system with fewer than ten passes or ten failures therefore gets a Clopper--Pearson interval at level $\alpha/(2K)$ in place of the bootstrap interval, and the maximal deviation is taken over the other systems and all costs at level $\alpha/2$ (at level $\alpha$, with Clopper--Pearson at $\alpha/K$, when every system is small, and with no Clopper--Pearson part when none is). The Clopper--Pearson half is exact whatever the data select, since each interval covers with probability at least $1-\alpha/(2K)$ whether or not its system is small, so the $K$ of them fail together with probability at most $\alpha/2$. The maximal-deviation half is asymptotic, and the proposition covers it when every system is regular, with expected passes and failures both tending to infinity, so that no system is small with probability tending to one. For a sparse system, or one whose expected passes lie near ten, the switch between the two intervals is decided by the data, and the total $1-\alpha$ is a design target supported by the simulations (Table~\ref{tab:valfrontier}), not a theorem. The low-accuracy boards of HAL (AssistantBench, SciCode) are the case this is for.
Nothing in the proof uses the cost order, so the band holds where the frontier set of Section~\ref{sec:set} needs the order to be recoverable. The price is width: a system whose cost is uncertain is allowed into the upper edge long before it is into the lower one. The band is a statement about the curve, the set a statement about its members; the two use different statistics and neither implies the other. The band is what to draw on a leaderboard page, Figure~\ref{fig:frontiers}; its width on HAL, as a median over the evaluated costs, runs from \NumHalBandWidthMin{} points (\NumHalBandWidthMinName) to \NumHalBandWidthMax{} points (\NumHalBandWidthMaxName), and in simulation it covers the true curve \NumValBandCovMin{} to \NumValBandCovMax{} of the time in all \NumValScenarios{} scenarios, including fixed costs, nearly tied costs and the low-accuracy board (Tables~\ref{tab:valfrontier} and~\ref{tab:realism}). The coverage is above the nominal level on most boards because the band takes one maximal deviation over all $2K$ means for a curve that depends on one maximum at each cost, so it is conservative by construction. A first version of the band that trusted the sample cost order covered the true curve only \NumValBandNaiveCovMin{} to \NumValBandNaiveCovMax\% of the time on those boards, which is why the construction above lets a system into an edge only on the strength of its cost interval.


\subsection{A confidence set for the frontier}
\label{sec:set}
Call $j$ a \emph{rival} of $i$ when $c_j\le c_i$ and $j\ne i$: a rival is any system that could dominate $i$ on cost. For each pair let $\hat\se_{ij}$ be the paired standard error of $\bar y_j-\bar y_i$, computed from the task-level covariance of the two outcome series, and let
\[
G_{ij}=\frac{\bar y_j-\bar y_i}{\hat\se_{ij}}
\]
be how far $j$ sits above $i$ in standard-error units. Two systems with identical outcomes on every task have a zero paired standard error and a zero gap; we set the standard error to $10^{-12}$, so that $G_{ij}=0$ and the pair never excludes a system. System $i$ is on the observed frontier exactly when $G_{ij}<0$ for every rival (with the tie conventions). The confidence set keeps $i$ unless some rival beats it by more than a simultaneous margin:
\[
\hat F_\alpha=\Bigl\{\,i:\ \max_{j\ \text{rival of}\ i} G_{ij}\le c_\alpha\Bigr\}.
\]
The margin is a bootstrap quantile. Resample the $T$ tasks with replacement $B$ times; in resample $b$ write $\bar y_k^{(b)}$ for the accuracies and, when costs are per task, $\bar c_k^{(b)}$ for the costs, so that the rival sets are resampled too. Let
\[
M_i^{(b)}=\max_{j\ \text{rival of}\ i\ \text{in resample}\ b}\ \frac{(\bar y_j^{(b)}-\bar y_i^{(b)})-(\bar y_j-\bar y_i)}{\hat\se_{ij}},
\]
the largest centred, studentised deviation of any rival from $i$, and let $M^{(b)}=\max_i M_i^{(b)}$ be the largest such deviation on the board. The margin is the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of $M^{(b)}$ over resamples, $c_\alpha=\max(0,\,q_{1-\alpha}(M))$. This is Hsu's multiple-comparisons-with-the-best construction \citep{hsu1981mcb}, as implemented for the confidence set of the best in \citet{opoku2026records}, with the rival set of each system cut down to the systems that cost no more and the margin taken jointly over systems. Two properties are immediate: $\hat F_\alpha$ always contains the observed frontier, because a frontier member has every $G_{ij}<0\le c_\alpha$; and it shrinks toward the observed frontier as $T$ grows, because the margin is in standard-error units.

\begin{proposition}[the set contains the true frontier]
\label{prop:retention}
Fix the costs. Let the task vectors be independent and identically distributed with finite variances, with the population variance of $y_{jt}-y_{it}$ positive for every pair compared, and let $K$ be fixed as $T\to\infty$. Let $F$ be the set of systems that no rival beats in true accuracy, the $i$ with $\mu_j\le\mu_i$ for every rival $j$; it contains the true frontier of Section~\ref{sec:setting}, which also drops a system tied in true accuracy with a strictly cheaper one. Then
\[
\Pr\bigl(F\subseteq\hat F_\alpha\bigr)\ \ge\ 1-\alpha-o(1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $i\in F$ and any rival $j$, $\mu_j-\mu_i\le0$, so $G_{ij}\le Z_{ij}:=\{(\bar y_j-\bar y_i)-(\mu_j-\mu_i)\}/\hat\se_{ij}$, and therefore $\max_{i\in F}\max_j G_{ij}\le\max_{i\in F}\max_j Z_{ij}\le\max_i\max_j Z_{ij}=:Z$, each maximum over the system's rivals, sets that do not change with the data when costs are fixed. By the multivariate central limit theorem the centred, scaled mean vector is asymptotically Gaussian, and the nonparametric bootstrap of the mean vector is consistent \citep{bickel1981bootstrap}. $Z$ and $M$ both divide by the sample standard errors $\hat\se_{ij}$, which converge in probability to their population values, so by Slutsky's theorem and the continuous mapping theorem $Z$ and the bootstrap $M$ share the limit law of the maximum of the coordinates of a Gaussian vector with positive coordinate variances, whose distribution function is continuous and strictly increasing; hence $c_\alpha$ converges to that law's $(1-\alpha)$ quantile and $\Pr(Z>c_\alpha)\to\alpha$. Hence $\Pr(F\not\subseteq\hat F_\alpha)=\Pr(\max_{i\in F}\max_j G_{ij}>c_\alpha)\le\Pr(Z>c_\alpha)\le\alpha+o(1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This is a confidence set for the frontier as a set: with probability about $1-\alpha$ every truly undominated system is kept at once. Two smaller sets with weaker, per-system guarantees come from the same resamples: the largest per-system quantile, $\max_i q_{1-\alpha}(M_i)$, as the margin, and each system's own quantile $q_{1-\alpha}(M_i)$; each keeps a given truly undominated system with probability about $1-\alpha$. Section~\ref{sec:validation} reports all three against the alternatives a practitioner might reach for instead, and on HAL the three sets hold \NumHalFrontierSet{}, \NumHalBoardwideSet{} and \NumHalPerSystemSet{} systems. The joint set is the one the rest of the paper calls the frontier set.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[per-task costs]
\label{prop:randomcost}
Let costs be measured per task, with the task vectors of outcomes and costs i.i.d., every accuracy and cost having positive variance and $y_{jt}-y_{it}$ having positive variance for every pair, and let the true mean costs $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_K$ be distinct. Let $F$ be defined from the true accuracies and true mean costs. Then the frontier set built with per-task costs resampled jointly with the outcomes satisfies $\Pr(F\subseteq\hat F_\alpha)\ge1-\alpha-o(1)$. (The band of Proposition~\ref{prop:band} needs no such condition.)
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With distinct true mean costs, the sample mean costs recover the true cost order with probability tending to one, and so do the resampled mean costs within every resample except a vanishing share, since each is $\sqrt T$-consistent for its target. On that event every rival set, real and resampled, equals the true rival set, and the statistics coincide with those of the fixed-cost construction with the true rival sets; Proposition~\ref{prop:retention} then applies. Ties in true mean cost are the case the proof excludes: a rival set that stays random in the limit. Proposition~\ref{prop:tierobust} below removes the condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
On HAL the per-task and fixed-cost versions give identical sets on \NumHalBoardsSetSameFixed{} of the \NumHalBoardsPerTask{} boards with per-task costs: cost differences between neighbouring systems are mostly many times their standard error, so on these boards cost uncertainty does not move the set. That is a fact about HAL's cost spread, not a general property, and the next paragraph shows what happens without it. Reruns of one configuration have the same true cost, which breaks the distinctness condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:randomcost} for those pairs in the harmless direction: whether the sample order puts a twin among a system's rivals or not, the twin is a true rival, so a wrong order can only withhold evidence from the set.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{When true costs tie.}
\label{sec:ties}
Proposition~\ref{prop:randomcost} needs distinct true mean costs, and near ties are what break it. Take a system that is truly cheaper than a rival by one per cent, with the rival truly more accurate. The system is on the true frontier, because the rival does not cost less. But when the rival's sample cost happens to come out lower, the standard set treats the rival as a rival and may exclude the system on its evidence. With true mean costs a few per cent apart and per-task costs with a coefficient of variation of 0.8, the standard set contains the whole true frontier in only \NumValScNearTiedCostsJoint{} of simulated boards (Table~\ref{tab:realism}), against the nominal 0.95. The same failure arises when a near-copy of a frontier system costs a few per cent less than the original (\NumValScCheaperNearCopiesJoint{}). The remedy is to exclude a system only when another system is shown to be strictly cheaper and strictly more accurate, and to use one critical value for both kinds of evidence.

The procedure is a step-down in the sense of Romano and Wolf \citep{romano2005stepdown} on two families of one-sided hypotheses, one pair for every ordered pair of systems. $C_{ij}$ says that $j$ is not strictly cheaper than $i$, $\gamma_j\ge\gamma_i$. $A_{ij}$ says that $j$ is not more accurate than $i$, $\mu_j\le\mu_i$. Let $D_{ij}=(\bar c_i-\bar c_j)/\hat\se^{c}_{ij}$ be the studentised mean cost difference, with $\hat\se^{c}_{ij}$ the paired standard error of the cost difference: costs move together across tasks, so it is much smaller than the sum of the two unpaired standard errors. $G_{ij}$ is as before. Start with no hypothesis rejected. At each step let $c$ be the $(1-\alpha)$ bootstrap quantile of the largest centred, studentised deviation over the hypotheses that still stand. Reject every standing $C_{ij}$ with $D_{ij}>c$ and every standing $A_{ij}$ with $G_{ij}>c$, recompute $c$, and repeat until nothing changes. The tie-robust set excludes $i$ when some $j$ has both $C_{ij}$ and $A_{ij}$ rejected.

\begin{proposition}[tie-robust set]
\label{prop:tierobust}
Let costs be measured per task, with the task vectors of outcomes and costs i.i.d., every accuracy and cost having positive variance and the paired differences of accuracies and of costs having positive variance for every pair, with no condition on ties among the true mean costs. Let $F$ be the set of systems $i$ with $\mu_j\le\mu_i$ for every $j\ne i$ with $\gamma_j\le\gamma_i$. The tie-robust set contains $F$ with probability at least $1-\alpha-o(1)$, and it contains the observed frontier.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal N$ be the set of true hypotheses and $W$ the event that none of them is rejected. A true hypothesis has a studentised statistic no larger than its centred version, so rejecting it needs its centred deviation to exceed the current $c$. Let $k$ be the first step at which a true hypothesis would be rejected. Every hypothesis rejected before step $k$ was false, so the hypotheses standing at step $k$ include all of $\mathcal N$, and the critical value $c_k$ is at least the $(1-\alpha)$ bootstrap quantile of the largest centred deviation over $\mathcal N$. By the multivariate central limit theorem and the consistency of the bootstrap for the mean vector \citep{bickel1981bootstrap}, that quantile converges to the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of the maximum of a Gaussian vector with positive coordinate variances, whose distribution function is continuous and strictly increasing. So the probability that a true hypothesis is rejected at step $k$ is at most $\alpha+o(1)$, and $\Pr(W)\ge1-\alpha-o(1)$. On $W$, an excluded $i$ has some $j$ with $C_{ij}$ and $A_{ij}$ both false, that is $\gamma_j<\gamma_i$ and $\mu_j>\mu_i$, so $i\notin F$. If $i$ is on the observed frontier and both $C_{ij}$ and $A_{ij}$ were rejected, then $\bar c_j<\bar c_i$ and $\bar y_j>\bar y_i$, so $j$ dominates $i$ in the sample, which is impossible.
\end{proof}
The proof uses nothing about how the cost and accuracy sampling errors depend on each other, but we also checked the set in a demanding case by simulation. Every system has the same true accuracy, so every system is in $F$ and any exclusion is an error; true costs are spread so that their gaps are of the order of the critical value; and in three of the \NumStressSettings{} settings failed tasks cost three times as much as passed ones. The set kept all of $F$ in \NumStressSdMin{} to \NumStressSdMax{} of boards, against the nominal 0.95 (supplement S12).

A \emph{gated} variant tests $A_{ij}$ only once $C_{ij}$ has been rejected. That shrinks the family and gives smaller sets, at most \NumValRobGatedExcessMax\% above the standard set against at most \NumValRobExcessMax\% for the set above. We do not use it, because the argument above does not cover it. With the gate the family of hypotheses at risk grows as accuracy hypotheses are exposed, so the critical value is not monotone across steps, and which hypotheses are exposed depends on the cost differences, which are correlated with the accuracy differences when failed tasks cost more. In simulation it kept the whole true frontier in at least \NumValGatedBaseJointMin{} of boards in every one of the \NumValScenarios{} scenarios, and in \NumStressGatedMin{} to \NumStressGatedMax{} in the demanding case, so the proof, not the evidence, is what is missing.

The price is size. When costs are tied more closely than their noise, no system is shown to be cheaper than another, nothing is excluded, and the set is the whole board. That is the honest answer: the data cannot say which systems are cheaper, so they cannot resolve the frontier. The set does not shrink to the true frontier when true costs are exactly tied, since a truly dominated system with an exactly tied dominating twin is kept at any $T$, the twin being almost never shown to be cheaper. Where costs do differ, the set is larger than the standard set by up to \NumValRobExcessMax\% in simulation. That excess is the price of not knowing the cost order for the pairs whose order the data do not resolve: they cannot be used to exclude a system, and the standard set uses them by guessing the order. The interval construction of honestboard 0.5.1 is more conservative still, up to \NumValRobIntExcessMax\% above the standard set. On HAL the tie-robust set holds \NumHalTieRobustSet{} systems against \NumHalFrontierSet{} for the standard set, and differs from it on \NumHalTieRobustBoardsLarger{} of the \NumHalBoardsPerTask{} boards with per-task costs (Table~\ref{tab:hal}). The paired cost comparison resolves the order of \NumCostSepHalMin{} to \NumCostSepHalMax{} of the pairs of systems on those boards, against \NumCostSepSimBase{} in the base simulations and \NumCostSepSimTied{} in the nearly tied scenario (supplement S7), so HAL's boards are not in the nearly tied regime. On CORE-Bench hard the set holds all \NumCorebenchHardSystems{} systems, a caution that the cost order there is only partly resolved.

\paragraph{An exact set for any number of tasks.}
\label{sec:exactset}
Proposition~\ref{prop:retention} is asymptotic. A finite-sample companion for fixed costs keeps $i$ unless some rival $j$ beats it in a one-sided exact paired test at level $\alpha/m_i$, with $m_i$ the number of $i$'s rivals: the sign test on the discordant tasks for pass/fail outcomes, a sign-flip test for rates, which also needs the paired differences to be symmetric under the null. A truly undominated system has no rival that is truly better, so under the task-wise null each rival's test rejects with probability at most $\alpha/m_i$ and the system is kept with probability at least $1-\alpha$, for any $T$, by Bonferroni over its rivals; dividing by the number of rival pairs on the whole board instead gives a joint version, which contains the whole true frontier with probability at least $1-\alpha$ for any $T$. With per-task costs the rival sets come from the sample cost order, a rival may truly cost more, and the finite-sample guarantee does not extend; the exact sets are then only a comparison, not a guarantee. The price is power: the exact sets are the bootstrap set's conservative cousins, and on HAL they hold \NumHalExactSet{} (per system) and \NumHalExactJointSet{} (joint) systems against \NumHalFrontierSet{} (Table~\ref{tab:resolution}).

\paragraph{What it would take to resolve a board.}
\label{sec:resolve}
A system that is in the set but off the plain frontier is one whose strongest cheaper rival leads it by $G$ standard errors with $G\le c_\alpha$. The lead in standard-error units grows like $\sqrt T$, so with $T'$ tasks it would be $G\sqrt{T'/T}$, and the system leaves the set once $T'>T(c_\alpha/G)^2$. Two summaries of this per board are reported in Section~\ref{sec:audit}: the task count at which half of the currently unexcluded systems would be excluded, and the task count at which a system trailing a cheaper rival by five accuracy points would be excluded, given the board's typical paired noise. Neither is a sample-size formula for a new benchmark, since the gaps on a future board are unknown; they say how far the present boards are from resolving the frontiers they draw.


\subsection{Two descriptive summaries}
\label{sec:summaries}
\paragraph{Bootstrap frontier stability.}
The share of task resamples in which system $i$ is on the observed frontier, with costs resampled when they are per task, describes how stable the system's membership is under the task process. It is not a probability that the system is truly undominated, and it is not calibrated: with 20 systems on 50 tasks it averages \NumValFreqUndA{} over truly undominated systems and \NumValFreqDomA{} over truly dominated ones (Table~\ref{tab:valfrontier}). We report it only as a stability measure.

\paragraph{The cheapest unresolved competitor of the best.}
\label{sec:cheapest}
The confidence set of the best \citep{hsu1981mcb} is the set of systems that cannot be excluded as the most accurate, with no reference to cost: the construction of Section~\ref{sec:set} with every other system as a rival. Its cheapest member, which we call the cheapest unresolved competitor of the best, answers the question a reader asks: which is the cheapest system the data cannot yet rule out as the best? It is not an equivalence claim. A system can be materially worse and still unresolved when the board is small, and non-rejection is not evidence of equality. Its coverage follows from the set's: the true best is in the set with probability at least $1-\alpha$, and then the cheapest member costs no more than the true best on the observed costs. That the true costs are ordered the same way needs the cost order to be recoverable, which the proof does not supply. In simulation the true best is in the set in \NumValBestInMin{} to \NumValBestInMax{} of boards over all \NumValScenarios{} scenarios, and the competitor's true cost is no more than the true best's in \NumValCpbOkOtherMin{} to \NumValCpbOkOtherMax{}, except when true costs are nearly tied, where it is \NumValScNearTiedCostsCpbOk{} (Table~\ref{tab:realism}).

\subsection{Extensions: new entrants and selected winners}
\label{sec:extensions}
A live board changes when submissions arrive, and a system reported as on the frontier was selected for being there. Two results built on the selective inference of the companion paper \citep{opoku2026records} address those facts. They are in the appendices so that the main text stays about the frontier as an estimated object.

\paragraph{Certifying a frontier entrant (Appendix~\ref{app:certify}).}
A new system is on the observed frontier exactly when it beats the most accurate system that is cheaper than it, so announcing an entrant is a record claim against one holder. The exact selective test of the companion paper certifies that record claim, with chain-wise error control over the board's history. The record claim is not a claim of Pareto membership, and we prove that no certificate built on the entrant and its strongest rival alone can hold its level against the composite null that some cheaper system dominates the entrant. Certificates of Pareto membership are given under a conditional null, in a normal-approximation form and an exact form for pass/fail outcomes. Walking HAL's boards in submission order, \NumCertEntrants{} systems were testable entrants, in that they beat every cheaper system on arrival, and another \NumCertCheapest{} were simply the cheapest so far with no rival to beat. Of the entrants, \NumCertSpending{} can be certified as record claims, and \NumCertIUSpending{} as Pareto members in the normal form, under alpha-spending over each board's chain.

\paragraph{The frontier winner's curse (Appendix~\ref{app:curse}).}
The reported accuracy of a frontier member is inflated by the selection that put it there. The corrections of the companion paper apply with the pool of cheaper systems in place of the board. On HAL they take \NumCurseMeanShrinkPt{} points off a member's reported accuracy on average and up to \NumCurseMaxShrinkPt{}.

\section{Data: HAL with per-task costs rebuilt}
\label{sec:data}

HAL publishes, for every run it hosts, an encrypted trace archive in its Hugging Face dataset (\path{agent-evals/hal_traces}), about 113~GB across \NumHalUploadsIndexed{} archives at the time of the audit. Each archive holds the run's configuration, its per-task outcomes, its total cost as HAL computed it, and the full call log: every model call the agent made, tagged with the task it served and the tokens it used. The decryption key is the public one in HAL's own harness. We read only the results member of each archive, a few hundred kilobytes to a few hundred megabytes, by HTTP range requests, and reduced each one on the fly to a compact record of outcomes, token counts and costs per task. \NumHalUploadsSkipped{} archives whose results member was too large to decrypt in memory on our machine (the largest 12.8~GB) were skipped; the other \NumHalUploadsRead{} were read.

\paragraph{Per-task cost.}
A task's cost is the priced token usage of the model calls logged under it, with HAL's own formula (fresh input tokens at the model's input price, cache reads and writes at the model's cache price, output tokens at the output price), one price table, HAL's current one, for every run, and each model call counted once. The last clause matters. HAL's reported total sums the usage block of every span in the trace, and an agent that calls a provider through a wrapper library logs two spans for one call, the wrapper and the provider call inside it, both carrying the same usage block. The effect was reported on the dataset's discussion board in September 2026 and confirmed by the HAL team \citep{liu2026halissues}; HAL's pages now carry a caveat, but the totals stand. Of the \NumDupRunsTotal{} runs we read with usage, \NumDupRunsExactlyTwo{} have a reported token total of exactly twice the once-counted total, \NumDupRunsBetween{} fall between once and twice, \NumDupRunsAboveTwo{} above twice, and \NumDupRunsOne{} are unaffected; over all runs the reported totals overstate token use by \NumDupOverstatementPct\%. Because the doubling depends on the agent's software stack and not on the model, it can change the cost order of agents on the same board; on the boards where it changes HAL's frontier it moves \NumFlipCounting{} systems (Table~\ref{tab:flips}). We drop the duplicate spans (a call whose parent carries an identical usage block, or whose children's usage sums to its own), and the supplement lists the ratio for every run. As a check, our per-task costs sum to HAL's reported total to the cent on runs without nested spans that were made after the last price change of the models they used, and to exactly half of it on runs where every provider call was wrapped. Over all \NumRecountUnnested{} kept runs with no nested spans, the recount matches HAL's total within a cent plus half a per cent on \NumRecountMatch{}; the other \NumRecountOff{} differ by the price date. Where the two differ otherwise it is because HAL's total embeds the prices in force when the run was made: an o3 run from May 2025 reports several times what the current table gives for the same tokens, o3's price having fallen by four fifths in June 2025, and an early CORE-Bench run priced cached tokens at the full input rate. A frontier compares systems with one another, so it needs one price list and one way of counting; we reprice and recount everything and report HAL's figure beside ours in the released tables. The supplement lists every archive with HAL's total, the ratio of token totals, our recount and the run's status. The two effects compound: over the \NumCostRatioRuns{} runs costing at least a dollar, HAL's reported totals sum to \$\NumCostReportedTotal{} against \$\NumCostRecountedTotal{} once counted and repriced, the median run is overstated by a factor of \NumCostRatioMedian{}, \NumCostRatioAboveOneAndHalf{} runs by more than half, and the largest gap is a factor of \NumCostRatioMax{}.

\paragraph{Outcomes and task sets.}
Outcomes come from the run's lists of successful and failed tasks or from its per-task evaluation results. A benchmark's task set is the tasks with an outcome in at least half of its runs; a run's task without a logged outcome counts as a fail, which touches \NumHalSystemsMissingOutcome{} of the \NumHalRunsKept{} kept runs, each missing at most 5\% of its tasks. Reruns of one configuration and HAL's test uploads of the browser agents are separate systems, as HAL lists them; they are each other's rivals at similar cost (a median factor of \NumRerunCostRatioMedian{} apart, Section~\ref{sec:limits}) and add to the dominated-on-arrival count of Section~\ref{sec:audit}. On ColBench backend programming HAL's accuracy is the share of tasks scored exactly one, and we use that; on ColBench frontend design HAL's metric is a continuous similarity score in $[0,1]$, and we keep it, so "accuracy" there is a mean score.

\paragraph{Exclusions.}
Of the \NumHalRunsRead{} run records, \NumHalRunsKept{} enter the audit. \NumHalDroppedCoverage{} runs were dropped because they carried outcomes for fewer than 95\% of the benchmark's tasks, and \NumHalDroppedNoCalls{} because their call logs carried no model call on more than half of their tasks (for instance runs of Claude Code, which does not log through HAL's tracer), so that their cost is unknown rather than zero; a system with an unknown cost cannot be placed on a cost-accuracy frontier, and dropping it is the only honest option. The supplement lists every dropped and skipped run. A kept run's tasks that have an outcome but no logged model call, a logging gap or a run that failed before its first call, are priced at the run's mean cost over its costed tasks rather than at zero, since a zero would push the run toward the frontier: \NumHalSystemsUncosted{} kept runs have at least one such task, \NumHalSystemsUncostedOverTenPct{} have more than a tenth, and \NumHalUncostedTasksPct\% of all system-task pairs are imputed; the supplement gives the count per system. On Online-Mind2Web the browser agents' logs carry a call record per step but no token usage on any call, so no per-task cost exists for any run there and there are no per-call usage blocks to double count; the run totals, which HAL computed from the stored run-level usage, reprice exactly under the current table (except for two o3 runs, for the reason above), and we use each run's repriced total spread evenly over its tasks. On that board cost is therefore a fixed number per system, not resampled, and it is marked with a dagger in every table and figure.

\paragraph{Why only HAL.}
Terminal-Bench 2 publishes cost for three of 244 job files and no per-task results, and SWE-bench Verified's cost metadata covers 47 submissions without per-task results. Neither supports paired inference, so the audit rests on HAL's \NumHalBoards{} benchmarks, with per-task outcomes and per-task costs for every kept run on \NumHalBoardsPerTask{} of them.

\section{A second leaderboard: HELM Capabilities}

To see whether the findings and the behaviour of the method carry over, we repeat the audit on a leaderboard of a different kind. HELM Capabilities \citep{crfm2025helmcapabilities,liang2023helm} scores language models on five fixed benchmarks, one request per task and no agent scaffold, and publishes for every model and instance the outcome and the number of prompt and completion tokens. \section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{Run-to-run noise.}
Each system has one run. The standard error of a single run is computed from the sample variance of its task outcomes, so it already contains the independent run-to-run noise of each task. What is missing is any excess beyond that: a run that is better or worse than its configuration's average for reasons shared across its tasks. HAL holds \NumRerunPairs{} pairs of runs of one configuration (same agent, model and settings) on \NumRerunBoards{} boards, which measure the excess directly. The two runs of a pair differ by \NumRerunMeanAbsDiffPt{} accuracy points on average and \NumRerunMaxAbsDiffPt{} at most, and in cost by a median factor of \NumRerunCostRatioMedian{} (at most \NumRerunCostRatioMax{}). If every task's outcome flipped independently between the two runs at the observed rate of disagreement, the root-mean-square difference would be \NumRerunRmsIndepPt{} points; the observed value is \NumRerunRmsObsPt{}, an excess standard deviation of \NumRerunExcessSdPt{} points per run. With \NumRerunPairs{} pairs this is weak evidence (chi-square \NumRerunChiSq{} on \NumRerunDf{} degrees of freedom, $p=\NumRerunP{}$), and it is not nothing. Carried to every board through the per-task disagreement rate and added to both systems of every paired comparison, an excess of that size would grow the frontier sets from \NumRerunSetBefore{} to \NumRerunSetAfter{} systems in all. If the excess is real, the audit's sets are lower bounds. The remedy is repeated runs, which the method accommodates by averaging them.

\paragraph{Task clusters.}
Tasks are treated as exchangeable draws. Where tasks cluster (several from one repository, one website, one problem family) the standard errors are optimistic, as in any task-level analysis. In simulation with 40 groups of five tasks that share a difficulty shift and a system-by-group effect, the set built by resampling tasks contains the whole true frontier in \NumValScClusteredTasksJoint{} of boards and the set built by resampling groups in \NumValScClusteredTasksClusterJoint{} (Table~\ref{tab:realism}). The module can resample task groups, but HAL's boards offer little to resample: SWE-bench Verified mini's 50 tasks come from \NumClusterSwebenchGroups{} repositories and USACO's 307 from \NumClusterUsacoGroups{} difficulty divisions, so margins of \NumClusterSwebenchMarginClusters{} and \NumClusterUsacoMarginClusters{} standard errors against \NumClusterSwebenchMarginTasks{} and \NumClusterUsacoMarginTasks{} say more about having two or four units than about dependence. Online-Mind2Web (tasks within websites) and ScienceAgentBench (tasks within source papers) probably have many small groups, but we did not group their tasks and so ran no cluster bootstrap on them.

\paragraph{The rest.}
Costs depend on prices, which change; we fix one table and say which, and a reader pricing a run under another table should rebuild the frontier rather than reuse ours. The recount rests on HAL's published call logs, and we reran no agent. HAL's team has confirmed the double counting \citep{liu2026halissues} but has not confirmed our recount as a whole; a request to do so is drafted in the repository. The HELM costs are recorded tokens at list prices, so they approximate a bill; they exclude the models whose reasoning tokens are not recorded, and they depend on the host chosen for the open-weight prices (Section~\ref{sec:helm}). The certificates and the winner's-curse corrections fix each system's cost at its mean, so selection through noisy costs is not corrected for. Systems may have been tuned on the public tasks, which inflates every accuracy on the board and is outside the model. Outcomes are taken as HAL recorded them; HAL has since disclosed that on tau-bench airline its Generalist agent's episodes that never terminated were recorded as failures, so those rows are lower bounds on that scaffold's accuracy \citep{liu2026halissues}. The guarantees of the band and the set are asymptotic. The band needs no condition on the costs. The set is proved for fixed costs and for per-task costs whose true means are distinct, and the tie-robust set removes that condition at the price of size. On Online-Mind2Web the cost is a run total treated as fixed. The set uses one margin for the whole board, which is conservative when the systems are many; per-system margins would give a smaller set with a weaker guarantee. Finally, the set is a statement about $K$ specific systems on $T$ specific tasks, and a new system or a new task changes it.

\section{Recommendations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Report the frontier set, or the band, beside the frontier. A board that draws a frontier from point estimates should say which systems cannot be excluded from it, at a stated level, how many tasks that statement rests on, and how many it would take to resolve it (Table~\ref{tab:resolution}: from about a hundred to several thousand tasks on the present boards).
\item Publish per-task costs and per-call token counts, count each model call once, and price every run under one named table with its date. The confidence set needs per-task costs to carry the cost uncertainty, and the audit shows that run totals alone can hide a several-fold difference in price between two dates.
\item Where agents built on one model have nearly tied costs, use the tie-robust set, which stays valid when costs tie and says so by growing.
\item Report the cheapest unresolved competitor of the best, with its level, beside the top system. Read it as a reason to run more tasks, not as a purchasing recommendation.
\item Certify frontier entrants with an error budget spent over the calendar, and say which claim is certified: the record claim against the most accurate cheaper system (exact), or Pareto membership (the composite test, under its conditional null), with costs fixed on arrival (Appendix~\ref{app:certify}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
A cost-accuracy frontier is an estimate. Treated as one, with a confidence set in place of a line, HAL's benchmarks say less than the lines suggest and more than nothing: on some boards the frontier is sharp, on most it is wide, and on a few it carries no content until more tasks are run. The tools here let a maintainer say which. They hold for tasks drawn from the benchmark's task process, conditional on the systems submitted, and they say nothing about tuning on public tasks or about new kinds of tasks.

\subsection{Ingredients from the companion paper}
\label{sec:ingredients}
Five pieces of \citet{opoku2026records} are used below, each stated here in the form needed.

\paragraph{The selective exact $p$-value.} For a holder $a$ and a challenger $b$ on the same $T$ tasks with pass/fail outcomes, let $n_b$ be the number of tasks $b$ passes and $a$ fails, $n_a$ the reverse, and $n=n_a+n_b$. A leaderboard tests $b$ only because $\bar y_b>\bar y_a$, that is $n_b>n_a$. The selective exact $p$-value is the tail of the sign test conditional on that event, $p_{\mathrm{sel}}=\Pr(X\ge n_b\mid X>n/2)$ with $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,1/2)$. For outcomes that are rates in $[0,1]$ the same conditioning is applied to a sign-flip test of the paired differences by Monte Carlo over 200{,}000 sign patterns.

\paragraph{The conditional task-wise dominance null.} The null under which $p_{\mathrm{sel}}$ is valid: on every task, given the two systems' outcomes on the other tasks, $b$ is no more likely to pass than $a$, with tasks independent. It is the natural "$b$ is not better than $a$" for paired data. Proposition~1 of the companion paper shows that under this null $p_{\mathrm{sel}}$ is super-uniform given the record event, provided the holder was chosen by an event that is increasing in the holder's own outcomes and does not involve $b$.

\paragraph{The per-date factor and the best-first order.} Submissions that share a date are ordered best first, so the challenger tested on a date is the best of that date, a second selection the record event does not cover. The $p$-value is multiplied by $g$, the number of submissions on the challenger's date, a Bonferroni factor over the date's members, which restores the guarantee.

\paragraph{Alpha-spending.} The $t$-th tested step is tested at level $\alpha_t=6\alpha/(\pi^2t^2)$. The levels sum to $\alpha$, so by the union bound the probability of certifying any null step over the whole chain is at most $\alpha$, for any number of steps and any dependence, provided each step's $p$-value is valid given that the step occurred and the step's level was settled before its outcomes were looked at.

\paragraph{The online fallback.} The $t$-th step is tested at $\alpha_t$ plus the level of the previous step when that step was certified \citep{wiens2003fallback,tian2021onlinefwer}, so a run of certified steps accumulates budget. The family-wise guarantee is the same, by a disjointness argument over the runs of certified steps that feed each null step.

\subsection{The certificates}
\label{sec:certify}
A live board changes when submissions arrive. A new system $b$ lands on the frontier when it beats every system cheaper than it; the maintainer who then tests it has selected it for exactly that, and a test that ignores the selection is biased. The frontier event looks harder than the record event because it is an intersection of events against several systems. As an event, it is not.

Let $C(b)=\{j\ \text{earlier}: c_j\le c_b\}$ be the cheaper-or-equal systems when $b$ arrives, and let $j^*$ be the most accurate member of $C(b)$, with ties broken by a rule that does not look at $b$. Then
\[
b\ \text{is on the observed frontier on arrival}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bar y_b>\bar y_{j^*},
\]
because beating the most accurate cheaper system is the same as beating every cheaper system (a tie with a strictly cheaper system leaves $b$ dominated, a tie at equal cost leaves both on the frontier, and $b$ counts as an entrant only when the inequality is strict). The frontier event is therefore the record event of the companion paper for the single pair (holder $j^*$, challenger $b$), and the holder was chosen by an event, "$j^*$ is the most accurate system in $C(b)$", that is increasing in $j^*$'s own outcomes task by task, depends otherwise only on the other cheaper systems, and does not involve $b$. These are the conditions of that paper's Proposition~1.

\begin{proposition}[frontier entrant]
\label{prop:entrant}
Fix the costs in advance of the outcomes. Under the conditional task-wise dominance null for the pair $(j^*,b)$ and independence across tasks, the selective exact $p$-value $p_{\mathrm{sel}}(b\ \text{vs}\ j^*)$ is super-uniform conditional on the event that $b$ is on the observed frontier on arrival. A rejection at level $\alpha$ certifies that $b$ beats the system that was the most accurate cheaper system when $b$ arrived.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[what the certificate says, and what it does not]
\label{rem:certificate}
The null tested is pairwise. If some cheaper system $k$ truly dominates $b$ while the observed best cheaper system $j^*$ is a weak relative of $b$ (the same agent with a smaller model, a lower effort setting, or a rerun, whose outcomes are correlated with $b$'s), then $b$ can beat $j^*$ with the stated guarantee and still be truly dominated by $k$. The test is not of level $\alpha$ for the composite null "some cheaper system dominates $b$". In simulation with such a near-copy as the apparent rival and a truly dominating independent system in the pool, a dominated entrant is certified in \NumEntrantNullCopyMin{} to \NumEntrantNullCopyMax\% of the cases in which it lands on the frontier, against \NumEntrantNullIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIndepMax\% when every cheaper system is independent of it (\NumEntrantNullR{} boards per case). The certificate is therefore a record claim restricted to the cheaper pool, and it becomes a certificate of Pareto membership only under an added assumption: that whenever $b$ is truly dominated by a cheaper system, it is task-wise dominated by the observed best cheaper system as well. Testing $b$ against each cheaper system with the pairwise selective test and requiring every test to reject does not repair this, because each pairwise test conditions on its own event and not on the whole frontier event. Conditioning every test on the whole event does, at the price of a normal approximation; that is the next proposition.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Pareto certificate]
\label{prop:pareto}
Let the vector of accuracies be approximately normal, $\bar y\sim N(\theta,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma$ the task-level covariance divided by $T$, and let $E=\{\bar y_b\ge\bar y_k\ \text{for every}\ k\in C(b)\}$ be the frontier event. For each $k\in C(b)$ let $p_k$ be the polyhedral selective $p$-value of \citet{lee2016exact} for $H_k:\theta_b\le\theta_k$ conditional on $E$, with the lattice correction of the companion paper. Then $p_{\mathrm{IU}}=\max_{k\in C(b)}p_k$ is super-uniform, conditional on $E$, under the composite null "some system in $C(b)$ truly dominates $b$", and rejecting when $p_{\mathrm{IU}}\le\alpha$ certifies at level $\alpha$ that no cheaper-or-equal system is truly at least as accurate as $b$: that $b$ is on the true frontier of its pool.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Under the composite null some $H_k$ holds. $E$ is a polyhedron in $\bar y$, so by the polyhedral lemma $p_k$ is uniform conditional on $E$ when $\theta_b=\theta_k$ and super-uniform when $\theta_b<\theta_k$. Hence $\Pr(p_{\mathrm{IU}}\le\alpha\mid E)\le\Pr(p_k\le\alpha\mid E)\le\alpha$. An intersection-union test needs no correction for the number of rivals \citep{berger1982iu}.
\end{proof}
Two things sit outside the proof. The covariance is a plug-in estimate, and the lattice correction is a continuity adjustment for accuracies that live on the grid $1/T$: it relaxes each constraint by half a step and takes the tail from half a step below the observed value, one part conservative and one not. The null rows of Table~\ref{tab:entrantnull}, at most \NumEntrantNullIUCopyMax\% and \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMax\%, are their check.
The two certificates answer different questions and have different prices. The pairwise test is exact for any $T$ and asks whether $b$ beat the system it displaced; the composite test is exact only under normality with a plug-in covariance and asks whether $b$ beats every cheaper system at once, conditioning on an event that truncates the statistic more. Table~\ref{tab:entrantnull} shows both on the three simulated worlds: with a near-copy rival the pairwise test certifies a truly dominated entrant in \NumEntrantNullCopyMin{} to \NumEntrantNullCopyMax\% of the cases in which it lands on the frontier, the composite test in \NumEntrantNullIUCopyMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIUCopyMax\%; with independent rivals the pairwise test certifies \NumEntrantNullIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIndepMax\% and the composite test \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMax\%; and when the entrant is truly better than every cheaper system by half a logit the pairwise test certifies it \NumEntrantPowerMin{} to \NumEntrantPowerMax\% of the time and the composite test \NumEntrantPowerIUMin{} to \NumEntrantPowerIUMax\%. The composite test gives up nothing in power for its stronger claim: the exact sign test is conservative because its statistic is discrete, which shows in its null rates (\NumEntrantNullIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIndepMax\% against the composite test's \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullIUIndepMax\%), and the normal statistic has no such step. What the composite test gives up is exactness. The audit reports both.

\begin{proposition}[exact Pareto certificate]
\label{prop:exact}
Fix the costs; outcomes are pass/fail. For $k\in C(b)$ let $\mathcal G_k$ be the information in the unordered pairs $\{y_{bt},y_{kt}\}$ and in the outcomes of all other systems, and let $H_k$ be the statement that tasks are independent and, on every task with $y_{bt}\ne y_{kt}$, $\Pr(y_{bt}=1\mid\mathcal G_k)\le\tfrac12$; equality is the sharp null that $b$ and $k$ are exchangeable given the others. Let $D_k$ be the number of tasks on which $b$ and $k$ differ, $N_k$ the number $b$ wins, $n_{11}$ the number both pass, and
\[
n_{0k}=\max\Bigl\{\bigl\lceil D_k/2\bigr\rceil,\ \bigl\lceil T\max\nolimits_{j\in C(b)\setminus k}\bar y_j-n_{11}\bigr\rceil\Bigr\},\qquad
p_k=\frac{\Pr(X\ge N_k)}{\Pr(X\ge n_{0k})},\quad X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12).
\]
On the frontier event $E=\{\bar y_b\ge\bar y_j\ \text{for every}\ j\in C(b)\}$: (i) under $H_k$, $\Pr(p_k\le u\mid\mathcal G_k,E)\le u$ for every $u$, with equality at the attainable values under the sharp null; (ii) if $H_k$ holds for some $k\in C(b)$, then $\Pr(\max_k p_k\le\alpha\mid E)\le\alpha$, for any number of tasks. For outcomes in $[0,1]$ that are not pass/fail the same construction by random swaps is valid under the sharp null only.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given $\mathcal G_k$ the unordered pair on each discordant task is fixed, so $N_k$ is a sum of $D_k$ independent indicators with success probabilities $q_t\le\tfrac12$ under $H_k$, and $\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12)$ under the sharp null. Swapping $b$'s and $k$'s outcomes on a set of tasks leaves $\bar y_b+\bar y_k$ and every $\bar y_j$ with $j\notin\{b,k\}$ unchanged, so given $\mathcal G_k$ the event $E$ is the event $\{N_k\ge n_{0k}\}$ with $n_{0k}$ a function of $\mathcal G_k$: the first term of the maximum comes from $\bar y_b\ge\bar y_k$, the second from $\bar y_b\ge\bar y_j$ for the other systems and is omitted when $C(b)=\{k\}$. Under the sharp null the law of $N_k$ given $\mathcal G_k$ and $E$ is $\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12)$ truncated to $\{N\ge n_{0k}\}$, and $p_k$ is the tail probability of that law at the observed value, hence exactly uniform on its attainable values. Under $H_k$ the probability mass function of $N_k$ relative to that of $\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12)$ is proportional to $e_n(r)/\binom{D_k}{n}$, with $e_n$ the elementary symmetric polynomial and $r_t=q_t/(1-q_t)\le1$; with $S_n=e_n(r)/\binom{D_k}{n}$, Maclaurin's inequality gives $S_{n+1}^{1/(n+1)}\le S_n^{1/n}$, hence $S_{n+1}\le S_n\cdot S_n^{1/n}\le S_n$ because $S_n\le1$ for $r_t\le1$; the likelihood ratio is therefore nonincreasing, so $N_k$ is smaller than $\mathrm{Bin}(D_k,\tfrac12)$ in the likelihood-ratio order, an order that truncation to $\{N\ge n_{0k}\}$ preserves; hence $\Pr(p_k\le u\mid\mathcal G_k,E)\le u$, which is (i). For (ii), $E$ is the same event for every $k$; if $H_{k_0}$ holds then $\{\max_kp_k\le\alpha\}\subseteq\{p_{k_0}\le\alpha\}$, and averaging (i) over $\mathcal G_{k_0}$ given $E$ gives the bound. An intersection-union test needs no correction for the number of rivals \citep{berger1982iu}.
\end{proof}
The $p$-value is the sign test of the companion paper with a higher truncation point, $n_{0k}\ge\lceil D_k/2\rceil$, and needs no normality, covariance estimate, lattice correction or Monte Carlo: only pass/fail outcomes, and that the null be read as conditional on the other systems' outcomes. That last point is the price. A system that truly dominates $b$ in means need not satisfy $H_k$: in the near-copy world $b$ wins every discordant task against the independent dominating system on which the copy passes, so $\Pr(y_{bt}=1\mid\mathcal G_k)=1$ there, and the proof does not cover that world. Table~\ref{tab:entrantnull} is the evidence for it. In the audit's three simulated worlds the exact certificate certifies a dominated entrant \NumEntrantNullExCopyMin{} to \NumEntrantNullExCopyMax\% of the time with a near-copy rival and \NumEntrantNullExIndepMin{} to \NumEntrantNullExIndepMax\% with independent ones, and a truly better entrant \NumEntrantPowerExMin{} to \NumEntrantPowerExMax\% of the time, on \NumEntrantNullRExact{} boards per row. The guarantee is conditional on the weak event $E$ ($\ge$); certification also requires $\bar y_b>\bar y_j$ for every $j$, which can only reduce the probability of certification.

\begin{proposition}[no pairwise certificate holds its level]
\label{prop:impossible}
Let $\phi_T$ be a test of the composite null that $b$ is dominated by some cheaper system, depending on the data only through the outcomes of the entrant $b$ and of the observed most accurate cheaper system $j^*$, and suppose there is $\varepsilon(m)\to0$ such that $\phi_T$ rejects with probability at least $1-\varepsilon(m)$ whenever $b$ wins every task on which $b$ and $j^*$ differ and there are at least $m$ such tasks. Then there are configurations with $\mu_b\in(0,1)$ in which $b$ is truly dominated by a strictly cheaper system, the frontier event has probability tending to $\tfrac12$, and the conditional rejection probability given the frontier event tends to one.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put in $C(b)$ a strictly cheaper system $k$ with $\mu_k=\mu_b$, independent of $b$, and a cheaper copy $j$ of $b$ whose passes are flipped to failures independently with probability $\delta_T=T^{-3/4}$. Then $k$ dominates $b$. Let $F\approx\mu_b\delta_TT=\mu_bT^{1/4}\to\infty$ be the number of flipped passes. Then $\bar y_b>\bar y_j$ with probability tending to one, and $\bar y_b>\bar y_k$ with probability tending to $\tfrac12$, so $\Pr(E)\to\tfrac12$. Given $E$, $j^*=j$ exactly when $\bar y_b-\bar y_k\ge F/T$; the left side is of order $T^{-1/2}$ with a continuous positive limit density at $0^+$ while $F/T$ is of order $T^{-3/4}$, so $\Pr(j^*=j\mid E)\to1$. Against $j$, $b$ wins all $F$ discordant tasks and loses none, and $F\to\infty$ in probability. For each fixed $m$, $\liminf_T\Pr(\phi_T\ \text{rejects}\mid E)\ge1-\varepsilon(m)$, because given $E$ both $F\ge m$ and $j^*=j$ hold with probability tending to one; letting $m\to\infty$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}
The class is not vacuous: any test that rejects once $b$ has won a fixed number of discordant tasks and lost none belongs to it, and the selective sign test is one. What lies outside it is a test that needs on the order of $\sqrt T$ wins before it rejects, the number an independent rival's sampling noise produces, and such a test pays for its protection by failing to certify a genuine entrant who beats $j^*$ by less than that. The convergence is so slow, of order $T^{-1/4}$, that the proposition describes the shape of the problem and not its size; Table~\ref{tab:entrantnull} gives the size at 50 to 300 tasks. A leaderboard that lists an agent twice, with a stronger and a weaker model or with two reasoning-effort settings, holds a pair of this kind in a weaker form: HAL's own reruns of one configuration agree on \NumRerunAgreeMin{} to \NumRerunAgreeMax\% of tasks, far from the asymptotic construction, which needs agreement on all but about $T^{1/4}$ tasks, and the simulations show what pairs that close do at realistic sizes. Any certificate of Pareto membership must therefore look at the whole cheaper pool, which is what Propositions~\ref{prop:pareto} and~\ref{prop:exact} do.

\begin{corollary}[a chain of entrants]
\label{cor:chain}
Let the step index $t$ count the dates on which at least one entrant arrived, and test every entrant of the $t$-th such date at level $\alpha_t$ (alpha-spending) or at $\alpha_t$ plus the previous such date's level when every entrant of that date was certified (fallback), with its $p$-value multiplied by its per-date factor $g$. Then the family-wise error of the chain of certificates is at most $\alpha$, in the sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:entrant} when the pairwise $p$-values are used and in the sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:pareto} when the composite ones are.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A date's level is a function of which earlier dates had entrants and, for the fallback, of their outcomes, and of no outcome of the date itself; it is predictable, and the null of each proposition is read conditionally on all earlier systems' outcomes. For a null submission $s$ on date $d$, $\Pr(s\ \text{certified})=\Pr(E_s)\Pr(p_s\le\alpha_t/g\mid E_s)\le\Pr(E_s)\,\alpha_t/g$, by Proposition~\ref{prop:entrant} or~\ref{prop:pareto}; summing over the at most $g$ submissions of the date, the expected number of certified null entrants on the date, given the past, is at most $\alpha_t\Pr(\text{an entrant arrives}\mid\text{past})\le\alpha_t$. The step index $t_d$ is predictable and takes distinct values on distinct dates with entrants, so the expected number of certified null entrants over the whole chain is at most $\sum_t\alpha_t=\alpha$. For the fallback, consider the event that date $t$ is the first date on which a null entrant is certified. Every earlier date whose level was passed along the run that feeds $t$ had all its entrants certified, and none of them null, so the run consists of non-null dates; the runs that feed different first-failure dates are disjoint, and the levels available to null dates sum to at most $\alpha$, as in Corollary~2 of \citet{opoku2026records}. Passing a date's level on when only some of its entrants were certified would break this: a date holding a certified non-null entrant and an uncertified null one would sit inside the run and have its level counted twice.
\end{proof}
Indexing each same-date entrant by its own step would make an entrant's level depend on its rank within the date, which is settled only after the date's results are seen; counting dates avoids this and spends one level per date.

\begin{remark}[random costs]
When costs are measured per task, $C(b)$ is itself random, and $b$'s mean cost comes from the same run as its outcomes (tokens are spent on failed tasks too), so the holder is not strictly free of $b$. For certification we fix each system's cost at its mean on the day of its arrival and check the consequence by resampling every system's per-task costs and rebuilding the chain: on the boards with per-task costs, an entrant keeps the same holder in \NumCertJitterStableMin{} to \NumCertJitterStableMax{} of \NumCertJitterR{} resamples, the number of entrants moves within \NumCertJitterEntrantsMin{} to \NumCertJitterEntrantsMax{} around \NumCertJitterEntrantsBase{}, and the number certified under alpha-spending within \NumCertJitterSpendingMin{} to \NumCertJitterSpendingMax{} around \NumCertJitterSpendingBase{}. The frontier set of Section~\ref{sec:set} is where the cost uncertainty is carried in full.
\end{remark}

\section{The frontier winner's curse}
\label{app:curse}
A system reported as "on the frontier" was selected for beating every cheaper system, and its reported accuracy is inflated by the selection, like a leaderboard winner's. The selection event, $\bar y_b\ge\bar y_j$ for all $j\in C(b)$, is a polyhedron in the vector of accuracies, so the conditional method and the iterated bootstrap of \citet{opoku2026records} apply with the pool $C(b)\cup\{b\}$ in place of the whole board. The conditional estimate is the median-unbiased estimate under the truncated normal law of $\bar y_b$ given that it is the largest of its pool \citep{lee2016exact,andrews2024winners}. The bootstrap finds the winner of the pool again in every task resample and takes its mean excess over its own observed accuracy as the bias; a second bootstrap level removes the bias the first correction leaves. Costs are fixed at their means, as in certification. A system with no cheaper rival, the cheapest on the board, has a pool of one and nothing to correct.

In the same splits, the reported accuracy of the frontier winners (\NumHeldoutWinners{} winner-splits in all) overshoots the fresh half by \NumHeldoutNaiveBiasMin{} to \NumHeldoutNaiveBiasMax{} points on average, depending on the board. The iterated bootstrap is within \NumHeldoutIterBiasAbsMax{} points of it and the conditional estimate within \NumHeldoutCondBiasAbsMax{}. Coverage cannot be read against a fresh half the way it can against a known truth, because the half-B accuracy is itself a noisy measurement: a correct interval covers it less often than its nominal level. The placebo column shows how much less, as the coverage of the same reported-accuracy interval for every system on the board, selected or not, at \NumHeldoutPlaceboMin{} to \NumHeldoutPlaceboMax{}. The winners' reported intervals cover \NumHeldoutNaiveCovMin{} to \NumHeldoutNaiveCovMax{}, the iterated \NumHeldoutIterCovMin{} to \NumHeldoutIterCovMax{} and the conditional \NumHeldoutCondCovMin{} to \NumHeldoutCondCovMax{}. Coverage therefore does not separate the corrected intervals from the reported ones on these boards, and the bias comparison, which does, is the evidence for the correction.
